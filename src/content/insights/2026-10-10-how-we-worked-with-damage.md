1.06%. The target was 0.8%.

That is how Damage looked in the April 2025 report. In last mile it was one of the main KPIs we followed every day, together with Incomplete, Delay and Loading Time. The figure was accurate, and it told us we had a problem. Where the damage had started, on the trolley, during loading, in the van or at the customer's door, it did not say.

This essay opens the series “Improvement that holds”, and an improvement that holds starts from what you know: how we followed damage, and which problems we saw.

## How we followed it

We followed Damage in the operational reports and in the daily stand-up. We analysed it alongside the other indicators and the everyday problems of the operation. I have two figures on record: April's, and 0.97% in a June 2025 report. Two reports are not a series, and I do not present the change as the result of what I did.

The problems we saw were operational: how the load was organised, how the trolleys were used, how the products were placed. The operation had standards for safety, for organising the ramp, for loading and for handling products. The Shift Leaders and dispatchers followed the operational problems, and coordinating with the DSPs was part of the daily work.

In [Pareto and 5 Why in practice](/insights/pareto-and-5-why-in-practice/) I wrote about the measures that came out of the loading cases in that period. Where most of the damage happened, I do not know precisely.

## A percentage has no address

Of everything in my work with damage, this has stayed with me most.

**A KPI tells you that you have a problem. It does not automatically tell you where it started or who caused it.**

A damage rate gathers into one number things that started in different places. A product placed badly on a trolley. A load organised in a hurry. A box that shifts in the van. A difficult delivery at the customer's door. In the report they all become one line.

Damage is also usually reported where it is found, not where it starts. I made the same point about mistakes in general in [Mistakes get lost between departments](/insights/mistakes-get-lost-between-departments/). With damage it is even easier to get wrong, because when a damaged product is found, someone always has it in their hands, and that person very easily becomes the answer.

If a manager cannot say at which stage damage starts, all they can work on is the percentage.

## Two ways to lower a number

In my opinion, a percentage can move without anyone understanding the process. You can put pressure on the team where the damage is reported and make it the topic every morning, and the number may come down for a while. But if the trolley is filled the same way, the load is organised the same way and the standard stays just as unclear, the damage will come back as soon as attention moves elsewhere.

The other way is slower. You follow the damage back through the process to the stage where it started, and you change something there. Managing damage should not only be an effort to lower the percentage in the report. It should be an effort to understand and prevent damage in the process.

That is also what the name of the series means. An improvement that depends on the manager's attention lasts as long as the attention does. An improvement that changes the process holds even when the manager is busy with something else.

## What I would do today

Today I would look first for the stage where the damage starts, and only then at the person or team where it is reported.

That does not remove individual accountability: if a clear and workable standard was not followed, that has to be said. It just comes second. For every damage case, I would look for four answers.

## One damage case, four questions

**1. Where did the damage happen?**
The stage of the process: preparation, the trolley, loading, transport, delivery. If the answer is “we do not know”, that is a finding too. It means the process does not record where damage starts, and that is where the work begins.

**2. Which standard was not followed, if there was one?**
If there was no standard for that stage, the problem lies in the system, which left that person to decide alone.

**3. Was the standard clear and workable?**
Judge it on the shift, with that day's volume, time and people. A standard that can only be followed when there is spare time is no more than a wish.

**4. What has to change so the damage does not happen again?**
A change in the process, with a person who takes it on and a date when someone checks whether it worked. “Remind the team to be more careful” is not a change.

Gather these four answers across many damage cases, and something appears that the percentage does not show: the stages where damage starts most often. **The goal is not a lower percentage in the report. It is one less damaged unit in the process.**

The percentage tells you how much. The questions tell you where. And only the “where” can be changed.
