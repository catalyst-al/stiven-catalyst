If I could give myself one sentence on my first day as a Team Leader, it would be this: once you have solved a problem today, ask whether it could be solved tomorrow without you. It is also the question this series closes on.

In last mile I worked with standards for loading, scanning, the organisation of the ramp and safety, and I ran into problems with procedures that were not followed and with drivers who were not yet fully used to the standards. In the hotel I organised the SOPs for Front Office and Night Audit. What I do not have is the case the title of this essay promises: a documented improvement of mine that held for a few months and was then abandoned. For an essay about improvement that fades, that gap is not a detail. It is the subject.

## The question I cannot answer

In 2025 I worked as a Team Leader and then as an Area Manager in last mile. Later I moved to remote coordination.

What was left of my way of working once I had left that role? Which checks carried on, and which were forgotten in the first week?

I do not know. I never measured whether the results continued after I changed roles.

That is what I would most like to understand today: whether a manager's results carry on when the manager is no longer there to check them. Without that answer, I cannot tell whether what I did improved the process, or whether it was only my presence.

## Why an improvement fades

What follows is opinion, not a proven case. I believe an improvement is at risk when it depends only on the person who started it, instead of becoming part of the everyday way of working.

Turnover makes the risk easier to see. In last mile we had drivers called in at short notice, and drivers in ramp-up who were not yet used to the SOPs. That made it hard to hold compliance at the same level.

A standard should work with more than the people who have known it for years. If every new employee learns the process only by watching a colleague, the result depends on who trains them. I wrote about this in [“Watch how I do it” is not training](/insights/watch-how-i-do-it-is-not-training/). Here you see its price: a standard that passes only from person to person loses a little with every change of staff.

## What keeps a standard alive

For me, no single element is enough. I would pick out five:

- **Documentation:** the standard is clear and easy to find.
- **Training:** people know not only what to do, but why.
- **Responsibility:** it is clear who follows up on whether it is applied.
- **Checking:** someone verifies that the standard still works.
- **Updating:** when the process changes, the instruction changes with it.

I connect this with organising the SOPs for Front Office and Night Audit, which I described in [How I built Front Desk Control](/insights/how-i-built-front-desk-control/). A good document is useful. But on its own it does not guarantee that the process is followed correctly. A standard that exists only in a document is not necessarily a standard that is applied.

## Ten essays, one question

Read together, the nine earlier essays seem to show the same problem from different angles.

[Damage](/insights/how-we-worked-with-damage/) and [incomplete orders](/insights/where-part-of-the-order-goes-missing/) show that we have to understand where a mistake starts. [Gemba](/insights/gemba/) is a reminder that the report does not always explain the process. [6S](/insights/6s-without-a-poster/) shows why visible order matters. [The control chart](/insights/a-bad-day-or-a-problem/) and [DMAIC](/insights/dmaic-without-a-black-belt/) teach us not to rush from a bad result to an untested solution. [Remote coordination](/insights/80-vehicles-you-cannot-see/) shows how much depends on reliable information. [High-volume days](/insights/high-volume-days/) show whether the standards are strong enough to hold under pressure. And [the work in the hotel](/insights/the-angry-guest-at-2am/) taught me how much responsibility and continuity between shifts matter.

The question that links them all is this:

**Have we built an improvement that becomes part of the system, or a temporary result that depends on the manager's attention?**

In my view, that question is the heart of the whole series.

## On my first day as a Team Leader

If I could talk to myself on my first day as a Team Leader, I would say: do not try to solve every problem yourself. Stay close to the operation, listen to people and understand the process before you change the rules. Do not judge a driver by a single negative KPI, and do not mistake a bad day for a systemic problem.

Above all, I would give myself the sentence this essay opened with.

You can improve a KPI, organise a ramp, write a procedure or solve a hard problem. A better result means the improvement has passed its first test. The others follow: whether the result can be held, whether new people can follow the same standard, and whether the process works without your constant intervention.

**A good manager can improve a working day. A good system keeps the improvement alive even on the day the manager is not there.**
