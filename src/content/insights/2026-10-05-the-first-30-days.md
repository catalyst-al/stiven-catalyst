On my first night at the new hotel, I could not work as a Night Auditor.

The system was running an update, and for several hours normal work was practically impossible. So I spent most of the night in the back office, taking in what I could about the hotel, its basic processes and the way the work was organised.

It looked like a lost night. In fact it taught me the first lesson: in a new place, you have to understand how the work is done before you do it.

This essay opens a new series. The first, *Ten years close to the work*, was about what I have learned. This one is about building an operation that keeps working when the manager is not there, and that begins long before you change anything: with how you read the operation. My material is the first weeks in that hotel: the checklist, the training documents and the colleagues who ran the night.

## The first nights: the people before the documents

In the first week my only aim was to understand how the night really worked.

In the nights that followed I concentrated on the Night Audit checklist, on the way my colleagues carried out the steps, and on the videos, screenshots and training documents, and I took notes.

I wanted to understand what had to be done, in what order and for what reason. A checklist gives you the steps. It does not always tell you why one step comes before another, or what breaks if you skip it.

At the start I stayed closer to the people who did the work every night than to the theory in the documents. The document tells you how the work should be. The people tell you how it is.

I learned a lot from the colleagues who actually ran the shift. One thing I understood only over time, not on the third night: in Night Audit the report is only part of reality. To see the real state you also have to look at the statuses, the open points, what the previous shift left you and what has to pass to the next one.

## The information was there, but scattered

What struck me most was that the information existed but was scattered: checklists, separate documents, screenshots, videos, and explanations that experienced people knew almost by heart. For someone who worked there every night, moving from one document to the next could feel normal. They had done it hundreds of times.

As a new person, I could see at once that the problem was not a lack of information. It was that the information was not always organised to tell you quickly:

- Where am I in the process?
- What do I need to check now?
- What comes next?
- How do I know I can carry on?

You see this better in your first days than after a year. By then you know the answers by heart, and you no longer notice that the document does not give them to you. That is why a new person's first days are a source of information most organisations waste.

It was also why I later began to structure the material into a [simpler, searchable SOP](/insights/a-good-sop-is-not-a-document/).

## Why I waited on purpose

At the start I decided to follow the existing process exactly as it was defined. The checklist told me what had to be done; the training and the videos showed me how it was done. I did not want to see something once and decide straight away that there was a better way.

First I had to understand the dependencies. A step that looks unnecessary from the outside may exist because another department, a report or a later process depends on it. If you remove it in week two, you may see no problem at all. The problem shows up somewhere else, later, with someone else.

Only once I began to understand the flow did I start to organise the information better. The aim was to make the operation easier to read, for myself and for the next person who would have to learn it. Changing it could wait.

**A step that looks unnecessary may be the step someone else depends on.**

## Three things for the first 30 days

If a new manager asked me tomorrow what to do in the first 30 days, I would tell them three things.

**First: do not walk in to show how much you know. Walk in to understand.**
Look at the process from start to finish. Talk to the people who do the work every day. Understand what happens when everything goes well, and above all what happens when something deviates.

**Second: compare the standard with reality.**
Read the SOP, the checklist and the reports, then look at how the work is actually done. Where there is a gap, do not decide straight away who is wrong. Find out why the gap exists.

**Third: build a map of the operation.**
What are the main processes? Who are the key people? What are the KPIs? Where are the dependencies? Which problems keep coming back? What can you decide yourself, and what has to be escalated?

And one thing I would tell them not to do: do not change something just because you want to show you have come to bring change.

## 30 questions for the first 30 days

In the first 30 days, a new manager needs 30 good questions more than 30 solutions. These are the ones I wish I had written down in my first nights. You do not need the answers on day one. You should have them after 30 days.

**The process**

1. What does the process look like from start to finish?
2. What result has to come out at the end of it?
3. Which steps depend on each other?
4. Where does the work wait, and why?
5. What happens when something deviates?

**Standard and reality**

6. What does the SOP or the checklist say?
7. How is the work really done?
8. Where is the gap between the two, and why?
9. Which step is skipped most often?
10. Which version of the process exists only by word of mouth?

**Information**

11. Where does a new person find the information?
12. How many documents do they have to open to carry out one step?
13. What do only the experienced people know by heart?
14. How does information pass from one shift to the next?
15. What is left open at the end of the shift, and who takes it on?

**People**

16. Who actually does the work every day?
17. Who do the others ask when they do not know?
18. What wears them out most?
19. What would they change, if they could?
20. What works well and should not be touched?

**Data**

21. Which KPIs or reports are followed?
22. Who reads them, and when?
23. What does the report not show?
24. Which problems keep coming back?
25. How is a problem recorded when it happens?

**Limits**

26. What can I decide myself?
27. What has to be escalated, and to whom?
28. Who owns each process?
29. Which departments does my work depend on, and which depend on it?
30. What do I need to understand before I propose the first change?
