A team can have people who speak different languages. The work standard still has to mean the same thing to all of them.

The subject is personal for me too. I came to Germany in November 2021 from a working life in Albania, and worked in operations where German was part of professional communication. I had to learn not only the language but also the way people communicate at work. I wrote about that move in [From Albania to Germany](/insights/from-albania-to-germany/).

The question here is the standard: how you make it clear to people who do not speak the same language at the same level.

## Language and ability are two different problems

**Not yet speaking a language well does not mean you are not good at your job.**

A manager has to tell a lack of professional ability apart from a communication barrier. They are two different problems and they need different solutions.

Treat them as one, and you either lose capable people because they do not express themselves well, or you carry an ability problem while believing it is only a matter of language.

That is why a work standard should not depend on someone's ability to follow a long explanation in a language they have not fully mastered.

## Short rules and visible steps

In last mile I worked with several DSPs and a structure of many drivers, dispatchers and Shift Leaders; in the hotels in Germany, with colleagues from different departments and with international guests.

The operational materials I used tell me that last mile had instructions and rules written in German and in English. They covered safety, hygiene, loading procedures, scanning and discipline on the dock. The rules were short and tied to clear actions: where the driver waits, when to inform the Team Leader, how scanning is done, what is not allowed during loading.

In the hotel I worked with checklists and SOPs in German, and with English materials for communicating with guests and for hotel services. I used checklists, screenshots, videos and colleagues' demonstrations.

When I was learning the Night Audit processes, it helped to see a concrete step on the screen and link it to the matching instruction. That also shaped the way I began to organise the SOP materials into a more searchable structure, as I wrote in [A good SOP is not a document](/insights/a-good-sop-is-not-a-document/).

## Translation is not enough

Translating a document is only the first step. The meaning and the action have to come through the translation unchanged.

Take a hypothetical instruction that says only “check the load”. Two people can read it differently: one checks the number of bags, the other the loading order.

In this example the fault may well lie with the standard, and an unclear standard stays unclear in whatever language you translate it into.

For me, a good instruction has:

- **one clear action** for each step;
- **short sentences** and consistent words, so the same thing is always called the same;
- **an image or screenshot** when the process is visual;
- **a way to check the result**;
- **a clear instruction for what to do when there is a problem.**

## “Show me how you would do it”

“Did you understand?” is not always enough.

People may answer “yes” because they think they have understood, because they do not want to look unsure, or because they do not know how to explain what is unclear. In a team with many languages, that happens even more easily. That is why I would rather ask:

**“Show me how you would do it.”**

Then I would check whether the person does it correctly, whether they know the result they have to reach, and whether they know when to ask for help.

I hold to it as a standard, and it is the same test I described in [“Watch how I do it” is not training](/insights/watch-how-i-do-it-is-not-training/): do it yourself, tell me why, show me what you do when an exception comes up.

When someone does not understand an instruction, repeating the same sentence louder settles nothing. As managers we have to check whether the instruction is clear, whether it has been demonstrated, and whether the person can carry it out.

## The clear-instruction card

A good standard is measured not by how well it is written but by how accurately different people can carry it out, even when they do not speak the same language. To test one, take an unclear instruction from your operation, “check the load” for example, and run it through these five lines:

1. **The action.** What exactly does the person have to do?
2. **Place and moment.** Where and when does it have to be done?
3. **The demonstration.** Which photo, screenshot or example explains the step?
4. **The check.** How does the employee know they did it correctly?
5. **The exception.** What has to be done when something does not work?

If you can fill in each line with short sentences, you have an instruction that someone who speaks the language less well than you can still carry out. If you cannot, you have found the point where different people will read the standard differently.
