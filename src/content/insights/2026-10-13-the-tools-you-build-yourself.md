Imagine a new employee at reception, in the middle of a process they have seen only once. The instruction exists, but it is somewhere between an SOP, a video, a few photographs and what a colleague explained to them two nights earlier. The process waits while they search.

The scene is a picture, drawn from my first weeks at the hotel, and it is the reason I began to build a tool of my own.

That tool is [Front Desk Control](/insights/how-i-built-front-desk-control/), which I developed in September 2026 to organise the work in Front Office and Night Audit: SOP instructions, checklists, information search and visual explanations with screenshots of the processes. Around it I worked on structures for Shift Handover and the night reports, on organising the Breakfast Forecast processes, and on checklists and step-by-step instructions. I use it myself.

The essay in which I described Front Desk Control presented it as work in progress. This one asks something else: what happens to a tool like that once it exists, and when it stops being only yours.

## Why the small lists appear

I built it because the information was scattered across SOP documents, colleagues' explanations, photographs, videos and checklists. I often had to search through several of them to understand how a process was carried out. The instruction existed; I could not find it at the moment I needed it.

I wanted a place where I could find the instructions faster, beside the hotel system rather than instead of it.

I believe most of the small lists beside the official system appear this way, out of a gap between the system and the work rather than out of distrust of the system. An Excel sheet, a printed page beside the screen, a page built in the evening. Each of them is a report nobody asked for, and it says one thing: here the information does not reach where it should.

**When an employee builds a tool to make the work easier, they may be showing a gap in the way the organisation distributes information.**

In [When a new system arrives](/insights/when-a-new-system-arrives/) I wrote that a document outside the system is not automatically a bad habit. This essay goes one step further: sometimes it is the best signal an organisation has of what it is missing.

## Useful does not mean authorised

The signal has another side. A personal tool does not become part of the official system just because it is useful.

A personal tool for organising my own knowledge and an authorised application that the whole team uses in operational work are very different things. The first is my notes in a tidier form. The second is something for which someone other than me takes responsibility: for its accuracy, for the data it handles, for what happens when it is wrong.

Front Desk Control has no official approval from management or IT, no formal test with colleagues and no company decision to use it, and I do not present it as a hotel tool. It is a tool that helped me. To become a team tool, it would have to pass tests that a personal project never passes on its own.

**A useful tool becomes a team tool only when it is accurate, up to date, authorised for the use it is given, and safe for the information it handles.**

## The data that does not go in

In the public version of Front Desk Control I required that no real guest data, reservations, payment information, credentials or confidential documents be included, and that the screenshots used for demonstration be anonymised.

I do not know exactly how every part of it works without internet, or whether all the data stays on the device, and I would not claim that a tool is fully offline or safe just because it opens in a browser. It is one of the easiest things to assume and one of the hardest to prove.

So my rule is this: real and confidential hotel information does not go into a personal tool without authorisation and without checking how it is stored. A tool you build yourself can hold the method. The real data belongs to the system that is authorised to hold it.

## The tool that depends on one person

Front Desk Control has gone through several versions. I have worked to update the instructions according to confirmed processes, to check that the screenshots show the right step, to separate the confirmed procedures from those that still need verification, and to keep the structure intact through each improvement.

But there is a limit I see plainly: nobody else is responsible for updating it when I am away. If only I understand and update the tool, it remains dependent on me.

It is what I wrote in [The tour the driver does not understand](/insights/the-tour-the-driver-does-not-understand/) about the experienced driver, only this time it is me. Knowledge that stays in one head is no less fragile for being written on a page that only one person keeps. And an outdated instruction in a tool that looks orderly is more dangerous than a missing one, because nobody looks it up twice.

## Five conditions before you share it

Today I would draw a sharper line between personal development and official use. For every tool created for work, I would require:

1. **A defined purpose and clear limits of use.** What it does, and what it does not do.
2. **Verification of the content against the official SOPs.** With the procedures that are still being verified marked as such.
3. **Approval before use with real data or by a team.**
4. **Someone responsible for updates and version control.** Someone other than me.
5. **Testing by other users** before anyone says the tool is ready for shared use.

Nor would I measure success only by whether the tool works technically. I would want to know whether it helps employees, whether the information is accurate, and whether it can be maintained without me. For Front Desk Control, measuring that is the work that remains.

## Before you share your tool

The card below is for every tool you have built for yourself and think could serve others too. Fill it in before you hand the tool over, not after it has spread. [Shift Handover](/tools/shift-handover/) is one example of a tool that holds the method and nobody else's data: handovers stay in your browser, and nothing is sent or uploaded.

Whether a small tool has become part of the work shows on the night its builder is not there: when someone else opens it, can trust what it says, and knows who keeps it up to date.
