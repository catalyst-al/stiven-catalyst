In [The night the system went down](/insights/the-night-the-system-went-down/) I wrote about my first night of training at the hotel, when an update left the system unavailable for hours. I had come to learn the processes and spent most of the night waiting. This essay starts where that one ended: not with the system that stops, but with the system that arrives, and with the people who have to learn it.

It draws on two experiences. In last mile, the move to a new system for calculating routes and travel times was planned for 19 August 2025. According to the information from that period, the aim was better planning accuracy and fewer unrealistic itineraries, and calibrations were planned so that the change would affect the drivers as little as possible. At the hotel, there were my first weeks of training: colleagues' explanations, the SOPs, the visual materials and the videos, and information that was not always in one place. Front Desk Control grew out of that period. For the August change I have no verified before-and-after comparison, and no record of resistance or of a return to old habits.

One thing needs saying. In those weeks in last mile we had problems with loading, missing bags, disorganised trolleys and delays, and I have them documented. I have no evidence that the new system caused them, and they may have had other causes. That is the easiest temptation when a new system arrives: billing it for everything that goes wrong in the first weeks.

## Two sides of the same door

In last mile I was part of an operation whose planning technology was changing; at the hotel I was the newcomer who had to learn a system that was already there. Side by side, the two experiences show the same thing: a system can be technically better than the one it replaces without people being ready to work with it. Readiness is not installed. It is built, or not built, in the weeks after installation.

**A new system does not become part of the work because it has been installed. It does when people understand it, know how to use it, and know what to do when it does not help them.**

## “How it is used” and “why it is changing”

On paper, the stated reasons for the August change were clear: better planning, more accurate times, fewer operational exceptions. I do not know whether they were explained to the team in a meeting or what the drivers and dispatchers were told, and I have no record of how I was informed myself.

I am convinced, though, that two things are often confused. Showing someone how a system is used is training. Explaining why the organisation is changing the way it works is something else. The first gives a person the ability to click. The second gives them a reason to report when something is wrong, because they know what the system was supposed to improve and can see when it does not.

The August change provided for collecting information from the field in the first days, and that information comes only from people who know why they are being asked. In [The tour the driver does not understand](/insights/the-tour-the-driver-does-not-understand/) I wrote that an experienced driver should not be the permanent correction of a system that does not learn. A new system is the best moment to change that, and the easiest one to miss.

## Information in five places

At the hotel, the difficulty I can confirm went beyond using the program. Learning relied on colleagues' explanations, the SOPs, the visual materials and the videos, and none of them had everything.

That is why I started [Front Desk Control](/insights/how-i-built-front-desk-control/): to gather the instructions, the screenshots and the processes in one clearer, searchable place. It did not replace the hotel system, and I have no measurement showing that it shortened the training. It was my answer to a problem I had myself as the newcomer.

**The difficulty of a new system is not only knowing where to click. It is finding the right information at the moment you need it.**

## The document beside the system

At the hotel I have worked with SOPs, checklists, Excel reports and instructions spread across different materials, all of them support for the processes. I have no case of an old list contradicting the system's data, nor of Excel being used to get around the system.

Nor do I think that every document outside the system is a bad habit. A checklist beside the screen may be exactly what makes a process safe. The problem arises when employees do not know which information is up to date and which is the official source. A new system makes that problem bigger, because every document that was correct the day before may now be wrong without anyone knowing. As I wrote in [A good SOP is not a document](/insights/a-good-sop-is-not-a-document/), the value of an instruction lies in being found and applied at the right moment. An outdated instruction is found just as easily.

## Six things before it arrives

When a new system arrives, I would today give priority to preparing the people, not only to installing the program. I have not led a change programme of that kind; these are the things I would recommend:

1. **Explain why the change is being made.** Not only what the new system does.
2. **Show what changes in the daily work.** For each role, not one presentation for everyone.
3. **Training with real situations, not only a presentation of the functions.** The ordinary case and the difficult case.
4. **Make it clear where to get help when something does not work.** At night as well, and in the first days as well.
5. **Collect the problems of the first days and give the employees an answer.** A reported problem with no answer is the last lesson people learn about reporting.
6. **Update the instructions together with the system.** Otherwise the document and the screen say two different things, and the newcomer does not know which to believe.

The fifth point is the one I would stress. Collecting information from the field, as the August change provided for, is half of it; the other half is that whoever gave the information learns what was done with it.

## Before the new system arrives

The card below is for whoever leads the change, and also for whoever receives it. If you are an employee and do not know the answers, ask before the first day. [Six Sigma DMAIC](/tools/six-sigma-dmaic/) has its Control phase for exactly this: how a change holds once the person who brought it has gone.

Installation changes the system. The way people work changes afterwards, through training, communication and daily experience: the ordinary and the difficult case practised before the first day, an answer to the problem reported in the first week, the instruction updated on the same day as the screen.
