The 2 a.m. in the title is not the time of an incident I can prove.

It is the time when the hotel has fewer people, fewer departments awake and fewer options. While the guest has the same problem they would have at midday.

This essay is about that gap.

## What I have and what I do not

From my work in the night audit I have concrete problems with rooms on record: weak water pressure in the shower, problems with how clean the bathroom was, technical issues that needed the responsible department, and open problems the next shift had to follow up.

I have no statistics to say which complaints came most often. I have no documented list of the decisions I could take myself and those that needed approval. I have no documented personal method for calming an angry guest. And I do not have a complete case where a guest came to the desk angry at night, what I said, what I did and how it ended.

I will not invent a conversation or a successful resolution. I use the technical problems here as real examples of the work, not as the story of a confrontation with a guest.

## What can be done at night

As a Night Auditor, I dealt with the problems that could be handled within the front desk's responsibilities. When a problem needed another department, it was passed to the person responsible. Issues that were not finished during the night were recorded in the handover.

In [When to solve it yourself and when to take it higher](/insights/solve-it-or-escalate-it/) I wrote about the line between initiative and authority at night. Here the same question has a third party: the guest waiting in front of you.

And that is exactly what makes the temptation bigger. When someone is angry in front of you, the urge is to close the situation as fast as possible. But I would not take a decision outside my authority just to close a complaint faster. A promise that does not depend on you does not solve the problem. It pushes it to the morning, and makes it heavier.

**At night, the options for a solution are not always the same as during the day. But that does not mean the responsibility for the problem should get lost.**

## Listening before explaining

For how I calmed a guest, I have no documented incident. But today I would stand behind this way of doing it:

1. **Listen to the guest without interrupting.**
2. **Understand what happened and what they are asking for.**
3. **Acknowledge their concern**, without promising something you cannot deliver.
4. **Explain clearly what you can do at that moment.**
5. **If the solution takes time, tell them what the next step is.**

An angry guest does not need to hear a long explanation of why the problem happened straight away. First they need to understand that their concern is being taken seriously.

Take the weak water pressure, which I have on record as a problem, not as a conversation. At night you cannot fix it. But you can listen, check it, record it accurately, pass it to the responsible department, and tell the guest what will happen next. That does not fix the shower. But it changes how the guest spends the night: not as someone who was ignored, but as someone whose problem has a name and a next step.

## A complaint is not closed at the desk

**A complaint is not closed just because the guest walks away from the front desk.**

In [Why the shift handover is one of the most underrated processes](/insights/why-the-handover-is-underrated/) I wrote about why the handover matters. For a complaint it has another meaning. If the guest has to explain the same problem from the start in the morning, the night's complaint was not closed. It was lost.

The shift report included problems with rooms, open issues and the information that needed following up. For me, a useful handover for a complaint should show five things: what was reported, what was verified, what has been done so far, what is still unresolved, and who follows it up, together with what the guest should be told.

That is the structure I recommend today. I do not know whether every complaint was historically recorded with exactly these fields.

## For the new receptionist

To a new receptionist before their first night shift, I would say: do not think you have to solve every problem that comes up during the night yourself.

Know your responsibilities, stay calm and communicate with respect. If you can solve the problem, do it to the standard. If you cannot, do not hide it and do not make promises that do not depend on you. And above all, do not leave an issue open without information for the colleague who takes over the next shift.

**Professionalism is listening, checking, doing what is within your remit and making sure the work carries on when someone else has to be involved.**
