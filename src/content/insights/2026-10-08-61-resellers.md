The first nine essays of this series were about people working inside the same structure: colleagues, shifts, departments, teams.

This last one goes back to Albania, to a network of people and businesses who were not my employees. They had their own interests, their own decisions and their own responsibilities. That is where I learned something that holds for the whole series.

As General Sales Manager in the wholesale distribution of household appliances, I was responsible for more than sales: the work included coordinating sales, the warehouse, accounting, technical service and customer support. In that role I built and managed a national B2B network of 61 active resellers. I do not have the figures for how many resellers there were when I took on the role, or for how long it took to reach 61. The development of that channel was linked to revenue growth of around 30 to 40 per cent, which I give as a link, not as proof that one caused the other. What follows rests on those facts; the lessons I draw from them are reflections, not measured results.

## A sale is a whole process

As General Sales Manager, I could not treat a sale as just closing an order. It was tied to the product being available, to the order being fulfilled, to the financial processes and to the service that came after.

That is why the commercial relationship had to be seen as a whole process, the same thought as in [Mistakes get lost between departments](/insights/mistakes-get-lost-between-departments/): a process does not end when I hand the work over.

## Partners, not employees

An employee works inside an organisational structure, with defined roles and responsibilities. A reseller is a business of its own, with its own customers, its own costs, its own priorities and interests. You cannot run a relationship like that by giving instructions alone.

**A partner is not managed like an employee. They need a reason to keep working with you.**

With 61 relationships like that, you cannot rely only on memory, on your personal relationship with each one, or on fixes in the moment. You have to think about how the way of working carries on.

What makes a network stable is a relationship that works without you personally in every conversation, however many partners it has.

## Trust as clarity and continuity

Trust in a commercial relationship should rest on clarity and continuity, and not only on a manager's ability to persuade the other side.

If I were building a relationship with a reseller today, I would focus on clear expectations, accurate information, communicating problems and keeping commitments. I would not promise a result I could not confirm just to secure the order. It is better to explain a limitation in time than to create an expectation you cannot meet.

## What links the resellers to last mile and the hotel

The most important lesson I take from this experience is that cooperation is not built on authority alone.

In Albania I worked with a network of resellers who were not my employees. Later, in last mile in Germany, I worked in an operation that included partner transport companies and drivers who were not all in my direct structure.

The situations are not identical, but they share one thing: the result also depends on people over whom you have no full hierarchical authority. Then it is not enough to say what the goal is. Each side has to know what is expected of it, how information is exchanged, who carries the responsibility, and what happens when the process does not go to plan.

The same logic holds in the hotel, where a process can pass through several departments. You cannot personally control every action, but you can help build a way of working in which responsibility and information do not get lost. Authority can help you make a decision. Trust is what keeps the cooperation going.

## The question that links all ten essays

This series began with the first night in a new hotel and with [The first 30 days](/insights/the-first-30-days/). Then it covered the briefing before the shift, training, escalation, mistakes between departments, KPIs, the conversation after a mistake, teams with many languages, and documenting processes.

Now it ends in Albania, with the 61 resellers.

At first sight, wholesale looks far from Night Audit or last mile. But the same question links them:

**Have I built an operation that works as a system, or an operation that depends on my presence?**

A manager cannot be the answer to every question, the solution to every problem and the check on every decision. If everything needs their presence, the operation may be active, but it is not yet independent enough.

Leadership, as I see it, is measured by the results I achieve when I am there, and also by the clarity, the responsibility and the ability of people to carry the work on properly when I am not around.

## The partner relationship card

The last tool of the series is for any relationship where the result depends on someone you cannot control: resellers, suppliers, transport companies, other independent partners. Five questions:

1. **The shared interest.** Why should this partner keep working with me?
2. **The expectations.** What have we agreed each side will do?
3. **The information.** How do we find out in time when something is not working?
4. **The problems.** Who takes responsibility, and how do we act when there is a deviation?
5. **The continuity.** Does the cooperation work without my daily involvement?

If the answer to the fifth question is “no”, you do not have a network. You have a few relationships that depend on you.

## Closing the series

From a network of 61 resellers in Albania to last-mile operations and hotels in Germany, I have worked in different settings with one shared challenge: organising the work so that the result does not depend on one person alone.

I have learned that documents are not enough unless they are applied, KPIs are not enough without understanding the causes, and authority is not enough without trust. A manager has to know when to step in, when to ask for accountability, when to listen, and when to leave people room to make decisions.

For me, the goal is a way of working that keeps running when I am not there, rather than a team that cannot work without me. A manager's value lies less in how many things pass through their hands than in how well things keep going when they no longer pass through them.
