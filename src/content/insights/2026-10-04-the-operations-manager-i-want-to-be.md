After years in retail, sales, logistics and hospitality, I have come to see that industries change much faster than the principles of management. The product can be goods in a warehouse, an order that has to leave on time or a hotel room. Systems change, KPIs change, even job titles change.

In the end, though, an Operations Manager almost always faces the same questions:

- Do people know what is expected of them?
- Does the process work?
- Does the data show what is really happening?
- Who owns the problem?
- And what are we changing so that the same problem does not come back?

Today I would sum up my working philosophy in five principles.

## 1. People

Operations do not work without the people who keep them running every day.

I have worked in environments with pressure, targets, delays, mistakes and moments when you had to react quickly. I have learned that a standard can be high and a demand can be firm without diminishing the dignity of the person in front of you. Pressure has its place. Humiliation never does.

For me, a manager has to be able to ask for accountability without using fear as a way of managing, because a team that is afraid to show its mistakes does not have fewer mistakes. It only has fewer visible mistakes.

## 2. Standards

I have learned not to put much trust in processes that only work because an experienced person “knows how it's done”. If the result depends on the right person being on shift, then the organisation has knowledge, but it does not yet have a standard.

That is why I believe in SOPs, checklists and clear ways of working. The test of an SOP is whether someone else gets the same result with it, even under pressure and with its author nowhere near to ask. How professional it looks in a document comes a long way second.

For me, standardisation exists to stop people reinventing what the organisation should already know, not to take thinking away from them.

## 3. Data

A KPI is a signal. The conclusion comes from what lies behind it.

I have learned not to be satisfied with a percentage being red or green. If delays go up, I want to know how many there are and why. If we have damage, mistakes, loading times outside the standard or another operational problem, I want to know where the problem is concentrated and what is causing it.

This is where ways of thinking like Pareto and 5 Whys help me. Not every problem needs a big methodology, but it is very easy to fight the symptom and call it improvement.

The data has to take me to the floor. And the floor has to help me understand the data. If these two worlds do not meet, the report can look good while the operation keeps having the same problem.

## 4. Ownership

For me, ownership means that no problem is left without an owner. I do not have to carry every one of them on my own shoulders.

The handover taught me this. A shift can work very well, but if the information does not pass to the next shift, the organisation loses part of that work.

The same goes for leadership without a title. You can take initiative, identify the problem, help and propose, but you also have to know where your authority ends.

So ownership, for me, starts here: I will not ignore it because “it's not mine”. And it has a limit:

**I will not make a decision that is not mine to make just because I am the person who saw the problem.**

That balance is one I want to keep as a manager, always.

## 5. Continuous improvement

My question often used to be: “How do we solve this problem?” Today it is: “How do we solve it, why did it happen, and what do we change so it does not come back?”

This change in the way I think is perhaps one of the most important professional developments I have made.

Operations improve through many small corrections, made all the time and grounded in the reality of the work, far more than through one big project a year.

## What I never want to do as a manager

I do not want to use fear as a tool for leading. I do not want to ask people for a standard that I myself am not willing to understand or respect. I do not want to hide a problem just because the KPI looks better without it. I do not want to become the manager who knows everything because nobody else has been trained. And I do not want the team to need my presence for every small decision.

If a team only works when the manager is in the room, then in my view that manager has not yet built a system.

## What my ideal week as an Operations Manager would look like

Every day I would start with a short overview of the operation, a few minutes long. I want to know what has deviated, what is still open, where we have risk and where my presence is needed.

I would check the main KPIs and then get away from the dashboards.

An important part of my week has to be on the floor: seeing the process, talking to people, and understanding the problems that do not always make it into the report.

Once a week I would want a deeper review of performance:

- What is improving?
- What is getting worse?
- Which problems keep repeating?
- Which actions are still open?
- Who owns them?
- And which problems are no longer incidents, but patterns?

I would also want dedicated time for people: feedback, coaching, and conversations where, after “what went wrong?”, someone also asks “what is stopping you from doing the job well?”

Reports tell me where to look. The floor tells me what is happening. A manager needs both.

## The first 90 days in a new role

### Days 1–30: Understand before you change

In the first month I would not come in with the idea that I am there to “fix” the organisation. I would learn.

I would get to know the team, the processes, the KPIs, the customer, the dependencies between departments and the way decisions are made. I would spend a lot of time on the floor and talk to the people who do the work every day. I would look at the SOPs and compare them with reality.

I would also try to understand what does not show up straight away on the dashboard:

- Where is time being lost?
- Which problems are already considered “normal”?
- What has to be done by hand because the process does not work well?
- Where do we have one critical person without whom the work gets stuck?

At the end of the 30 days I want a clear map of the operation. The list of things to change can wait.

### Days 31–60: Set the priorities

In the second phase I would start separating the symptoms from the real problems. I would look at the data and for the patterns that repeat, and I would choose a few priorities and leave the rest for later.

For each priority I want it to be clear:

- What is the problem?
- How do we know it is a problem?
- What is the likely cause?
- Who owns it?
- What are we going to change?
- How will we know whether the change worked?

In this phase I would also start the small corrections that can be made without destabilising the operation.

### Days 61–90: Turn improvements into a way of working

Three months are not magic, and by day 90 I would not claim to have transformed the operation. But I would want some things to have become clearer:

- The main KPIs and what they mean.
- The ownership of problems.
- The way the handover is done.
- The process for following up actions.
- The standards that are actually used.
- A regular rhythm in which the team checks its performance and its improvement.

My goal for the 90 days would be for the organisation to have a clearer picture of itself and some better mechanisms for improving. How much I had changed would not be the measure.

## Where I am still learning

There is a lot I want to develop further. I want to deepen my knowledge of data analysis and of how operations connect to the P&L, forecasting and financial decisions at a broader level.

I want to gain more experience leading operations with a larger organisational scope, where decisions go beyond one shift or one function and affect several teams at once.

And I want to keep improving at something I do not think is ever finished: knowing when to step in and when to let people solve things themselves.

My goal is to become the manager who builds a team where the answers do not always have to come from him.

## How I would like to be described

If one day I asked the people who have worked with me how they would describe me, I would not ask them to say I was the smartest manager in the room. This would be enough for me:

**“He asked a lot of us, but he was there with us, he listened to us, and he tried to leave the process better than he found it.”**

That is the Operations Manager I want to be: one who builds an operation that needs rescuing less and less.
