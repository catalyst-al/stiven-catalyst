You can have two shifts that are each very good on their own. The first may have done everything right, and the second may have people who are just as capable. But between them there is a point where the organisation can lose its memory. That point is the handover.

A problem that does not make it into the handover does not stop existing. It just becomes the problem of someone who does not yet know it exists.

I hold this as a principle from hotel work, where I have watched open issues, technical problems, requests that needed follow-up and operational differences pass to the next shift.

## From a loose note to the current state

The change I can name in my own way of working is the move towards a more structured handover.

Instead of leaving the information as a loose note, or as a list of things with no clear link between them, I have organised it by categories of work:

- What is the state of the hotel?
- What happened during the shift?
- What has been resolved?
- What is still open?
- Who has to follow it up?

This way of working has also found its way into the SOPs and the structures I later built for the handover.

For me the main improvement is that the next shift should receive the current state instead of having to rebuild the story on its own.

## What a night handover contains

Leaving the specific content aside, the handover I use or have structured around night work includes categories such as:

- the overall operational state of the hotel;
- the main movements of the day and those that affect the next shift;
- reservations or statuses that need attention;
- open administrative or financial matters;
- guest problems or requests that need to be continued;
- technical issues;
- information for housekeeping when there is something relevant for the next shift;
- open tasks and follow-ups;
- notes the next shift needs so it does not start work without context.

In my reports, a problem that needs follow-up has to stand out as still open rather than read as a story of what happened, because “reported” and “resolved” are not the same thing.

## A document the next person can use

The information has to be clear enough that the other person does not need to interpret what I meant.

A note like “problem with the room” falls short. The handover has to make clear what kind of problem it is, what state it is in and whether there is still something to follow up, without adding unnecessary information.

The way I have built the reporting makes one thing clear: the person who receives the document has to be able to work from it. Proof that the handover happened is not enough.

## Two moments that mirror each other

At the end of the shift my focus moves from “what do I need to do?” to “what does the person after me need to know?”

I check what has been left open, what was resolved during the night, and which issues must not get lost when the shift changes. I take particular care that an open problem does not look like a finished one just because my shift is ending.

When I take over the shift from someone else, I do the opposite and try to build a picture of the current state as quickly as possible: what I am inheriting, what is normal, what has deviated and which points need attention.

To me the two moments almost mirror each other. When I hand over the shift, I have to reduce the uncertainty for the other person. When I take it over, I have to uncover the uncertainty that has been left behind.

## What a tool does and what it does not

The handover tool I built follows almost the same logic.

The figures and the operational state give a snapshot of the moment. Open points separate what has happened from what still needs action. The owner matters, because a task without an owner is very close to a task nobody will do. The time or deadline separates information you only need to know from something you need to act on. The notes keep the context that does not fit into a number or a status. Security is a category too, but its content and procedure stay out of this essay.

No tool fully replaces the human context. A system can record that a point is open, who has to follow it up and by when, but it cannot by itself guarantee that the person taking over the shift has understood why it matters.

That is why, for me, a handover passes on responsibility as well as information, and this is exactly where many organisations go wrong.

If the information does not cross the line between two shifts, the good work of the first shift no longer belongs to the organisation. It stays only in the head of the person who has just gone home.
