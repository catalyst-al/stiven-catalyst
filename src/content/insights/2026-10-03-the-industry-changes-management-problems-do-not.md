I have worked in retail and wholesale in Albania, then in last-mile logistics, and today in hospitality in Germany. From the outside these are three different worlds. From the inside, almost everywhere, I have met the same problem: the gap between what the process says should happen and what actually happens on the ground.

In retail and wholesale you could have the goods, but if stock was not controlled, if the warehouse and sales did not communicate well, or if the information was not accurate, the problem ended up with the customer.

In logistics the same problem took another form: a missing parcel, a loading that took longer than it should, an incomplete order, a route that left late, or a problem that had to be solved while the driver was already on the road.

In a hotel it changes shape again: a reservation, a payment, a room that has not been closed properly in the system, a no-show, an invoice, or a piece of information that did not pass from one shift to the next.

The industry changes. The thing you control changes. But the management problem stays the same: if information, responsibility and execution do not meet at the same point, a mistake will show up somewhere.

I see this most clearly when I think about what “inventory” meant in each industry.

In retail, inventory meant what it classically means: the product, the warehouse and what you actually had available to sell. In Albania I was responsible for inventory, the warehouse, sales, costs and cash flow, and at one of the companies one of my tasks was to improve inventory management. Every count of products stood for the company's capital, and that capital had to match sales and real demand.

In logistics, inventory became the physical flow of orders. You had to check whether loading ran on time, whether parcels were missing, whether there were incomplete orders or damage, and whether the drivers left at the right time. The indicators I worked with were loading time, delays, incomplete orders and damage. As a dispatcher I coordinated more than 70 drivers in a shift, splitting routes, checking departures and solving problems as they happened.

In a hotel, inventory becomes more abstract: the rooms, the reservations, their status and the money attached to them. During the night I check reservations, no-shows, payments, credit and financial movements, and the rooms that should have been checked out or extended. In the end I check whether the day can be closed without leaving mistakes for the next shift.

I went from checking a box in a warehouse, to checking an order on the move, and then to checking a room or a transaction that often exists only as information in a system. The principle has not changed: what the system says you have and what really exists must be the same thing.

The customer changes too. In a shop they stand in front of you: you see them, you read their reaction, and you have more room to steer the conversation. In last mile the relationship is much shorter. The customer expects the order to arrive on time, complete and undamaged. They do not see the warehouse, the loading, the problems on the route or what happens behind the screen. For them the result is simple: the order arrived as it should, or it did not.

In a hotel, especially at night, people may arrive tired from travelling, with a problem with their reservation, their payment or their room. At that hour you do not have behind you the whole management structure that exists during the day. You have to handle the situation yourself, and also know where your authority ends.

What does not change is the expectation. The customer does not much care which department made the mistake. They only see the company in front of them. In the shop that is you, at the door the driver, in the hotel the receptionist. In that moment, for the customer, that person is the whole company.

Another lesson came from wholesale, and it has served me more than I expected. I built and managed a national network of 61 active resellers, and that channel was linked to revenue growth of around 30 to 40 per cent. It taught me that a partner has to be managed differently from an employee. An employee can be given a procedure and a direct responsibility. With a partner you have to build a shared interest, communication and continuity, and the relationship has to make economic sense for both sides. With 61 partners you cannot rely only on your personal relationship with each one. You need a way of working that repeats.

This helped me later, even when the people were no longer called resellers. In logistics you work with drivers, operators and partner companies. In a hotel you work with other departments, suppliers and structures you do not control directly. Management is not always having authority over someone. Often it is getting people you have no authority over to work in the same direction as you.

Operational control transferred easily from one industry to the next. Everywhere I have had the same instinct: what should happen, what is really happening, where the gap is and who needs to act. Working with people transferred too. Job titles change faster than human nature. Pressure, misunderstanding, responsibility and the need to communicate exist everywhere.

What did not transfer automatically was the technical knowledge of each industry. Knowing how to run a shop does not get you into logistics; you have to learn routing, dispatch, loading and how a live operation works. Hospitality asks even more. The property management system, reservations, credit, payments, no-shows, the night audit procedures and closing the day are a language of their own, and I had to learn it.

That is why I do not put much faith in the idea that a good manager has to know every detail of the industry from day one. You can learn the industry. What is harder to learn is to see when a process is not working, not to walk past the problem, and to take it on and see it through.

That part I have carried with me from one industry to the next.
