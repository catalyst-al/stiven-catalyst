I have worked in retail and wholesale in Albania, then in last-mile logistics, and today in hospitality in Germany. From the outside they look like three different worlds. From the inside, the problem I have seen almost everywhere is the same one: the gap between what should happen according to the process and what actually happens on the ground.

In retail and wholesale you could have the goods, but if stock was not controlled, if the warehouse and sales did not communicate well, or if the information was not accurate, the problem ended up with the customer.

In logistics, the same thing took another form. It was no longer a product on a shelf. It was a missing parcel, a loading that took longer than it should, an incomplete order, a route that left late, or a problem that had to be solved while the driver was already on the road.

In a hotel, the problem changes its look again. It can be a reservation, a payment, a room that has not been closed properly in the system, a no-show, an invoice, or a piece of information that did not pass from one shift to the next.

The industry changes. The thing you control changes. But the management problem stays the same: if information, responsibility and execution do not meet at the same point, a mistake will show up somewhere.

I see this most clearly when I think about what "inventory" was in each industry.

In retail, inventory was inventory in the classic sense: the product, the warehouse, and what you really had available to sell. In Albania I was responsible for inventory, the warehouse, sales, costs and cash flow, and at one of the companies one of my tasks was to improve inventory management. Inventory was not just a count of products. It was the company's capital, and it had to match sales and real demand.

In logistics, inventory became the physical flow of orders. You had to check whether loading was happening on time, whether there were missing parcels, incomplete orders or damage, and whether drivers were leaving at the right time. I worked with concrete indicators: loading time, delays, incomplete orders, damage. As a dispatcher I coordinated more than 70 drivers in a shift, splitting routes, checking departures and solving problems live.

In a hotel, inventory becomes more abstract. It is the rooms, the reservations, their status and the money attached to them. During the night I check reservations, no-shows, payments, credit, financial movements, the rooms that should have been checked out or extended, and in the end whether the day can be closed without leaving mistakes for the next shift.

I went from checking a box in a warehouse, to checking an order on the move, and then to checking a room or a transaction that often exists only as information in a system. The principle is the same: what the system says you have and what really exists must be the same thing.

The customer changes too. In a shop they are in front of you. You see them, you read their reaction, and you have more room to steer the conversation. In last mile, the relationship is much shorter. The customer expects the order to arrive on time, complete and undamaged. They do not see the warehouse, the loading, the route problems or what happens behind the screen. For them the result is simple: the order arrived as it should, or it did not. In a hotel, especially at night, people may arrive tired from travelling, with a problem with their reservation, their payment or their room, and at that hour you do not have the whole management structure behind you that exists during the day. You have to handle the situation yourself, and also know where your authority ends.

What does not change is the expectation. The customer does not much care which department made the mistake. They only see the company in front of them. In the shop it is you. At the door it is the driver. In the hotel it is the receptionist. In that moment, for the customer, that person is the whole company.

Another lesson came from wholesale, and it has served me more than I expected. I built and managed a national network of 61 active resellers, and that channel was linked to revenue growth of around 30 to 40 percent. That experience taught me that a partner is not managed like an employee. You can give an employee a procedure and a direct responsibility. With a partner you have to create a shared interest, communication and continuity, and the relationship has to work economically for both sides. When you have 61 partners, you cannot rely only on your personal relationship with each one. You need a way of working that repeats.

This helped me later, even when the people were no longer called resellers. In logistics you work with drivers, operators and partner companies. In a hotel you work with other departments, suppliers and structures you do not control directly. Management is not always having authority over someone. Often it is getting people you have no authority over to work in the same direction as you.

What transferred easily from one industry to the next? Operational control. Everywhere I have had the same instinct: what should happen, what is really happening, where the gap is, and who needs to act. Working with people transferred too. Job titles change faster than human nature. Pressure, misunderstanding, responsibility and the need to communicate exist everywhere.

What did not transfer automatically was the technical knowledge of the industry. You cannot walk into logistics just because you know how to run a shop. You have to learn routing, dispatch, loading and how a live operation works. And even more so in hospitality: the property management system, reservations, credit, payments, no-shows, the night audit procedures and closing the day are a language of their own. I had to learn that language.

That is why today I do not put much faith in the idea that a good manager has to know every detail of the industry from day one. You can learn the industry. What is harder to learn is to see when a process is not working, not to walk past the problem, and to take it on until the end.

That part I have carried with me from one industry to the next.
