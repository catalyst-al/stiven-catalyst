My question used to be: “How do we solve this?”

Today it is longer: “How do we solve this, who owns it afterwards, and what do we change so it does not come back?” That may be the biggest change I have made as a leader.

## How I led in Albania

In Albania I held roles with broad management responsibility. I was close to sales, inventory, the warehouse, costs, cash flow and the development of the partner network, and I managed a network of 61 active resellers.

That meant many problems could not be seen from the point of view of a single department. A sales decision affected stock, a stock problem reached the customer, and a weak relationship with a partner showed up in revenue.

My style was more direct and strongly focused on the solution. When a problem came up, my instinct was to get inside it, understand what was happening and push us to move quickly towards a fix.

With the team, too, I always preferred to stay close to the operation. I never liked the idea of a manager who knows the report but not what happens where the work is done.

## The weight of the process

In Germany, what has become far more visible to me is the weight of the process.

In many of the roles I have had here, it is not enough for the work to be done. It has to be done according to the process, it has to be documented and, when necessary, it has to be possible to prove that it was checked.

I have seen this in logistics and in hospitality alike. In logistics there were KPIs, a clear split of responsibilities, procedures, operating rules and escalation whenever something went outside the standard. In hospitality it is even more visible: checklists, reports, shift handovers, checks, and much sharper limits between what you can decide yourself and what you have to escalate.

Documentation also carries weight. A problem only one person knows about is still a problem for the organisation. Once it is documented, it can be followed up and given an owner, and you can see whether it is repeating.

## What I took from Germany

The biggest change is process discipline.

I used to focus mainly on the result: the problem exists, so it has to be solved. Today I add a second question: what has to change in the process so that we do not have to solve the same problem again?

That is why I now give far more weight to SOPs, checklists, KPIs, the handover, documentation and the ownership of each task.

I have also learned to separate initiative from authority. I can identify a problem, analyse it, propose a solution and do my part, but that does not make every decision mine. This line matters much more to me today than it used to.

And I have learned something that can look bureaucratic at first but is worth a great deal in operations: if a process only works because a good person remembers it, you do not yet have a good process.

## What I kept from Albania

I have kept the habit of staying close to the operation. I still do not believe you can lead a process well from reports alone; you have to see what happens where the work is done. In logistics that means knowing what happens on the ramp and with the people during the shift, not only the percentage at the end of the day. In a hotel it means knowing what happens at the reception desk during a shift, not only reading the report in the morning.

I have kept my flexibility too. When a problem comes up, I do not like to answer straight away with “That's not my job.” First I try to see whether I can help without crossing the limits of my role.

And I have kept the human relationship with the team. Process matters, but people are not processes. An employee can have the same task as yesterday and come to work today in a completely different state. Management has to see that as well.

I would not say these things are “missing” in Germany; my own experience could not support that generalisation. They are parts of my style that I brought with me and did not want to lose while adapting to a more structured working culture.

## What I would do differently

What I would change is not one decision from Albania that I could reconstruct with certainty, but a habit. Today I would document more.

If a process worked because I knew it, today I would try to make the system know it. If a problem was solved, it would not be enough for me that it went away. I would want to understand why it happened and whether something in the process has to change.

I would also separate personal responsibility from organisational responsibility earlier. It is not always good for a leader to become the person who solves everything, because the organisation can start to depend on that person instead of improving the way it works.

## Two strengths that complement each other

I do not think one working culture should copy the other. From what I have lived, though, I see two strengths that can complement each other well.

From the structured way of working I have experienced in Germany, you can take more process discipline: clearer standards, documentation, defined roles, measurement and continuity. That makes an organisation less dependent on the individual.

From my experience in Albania, I would keep the speed of reaction, the closeness to people and the ability to adapt when reality does not match the plan. Even the best process on paper will one day meet a situation it does not describe.

My way of leading today sits between these two worlds. From one I took the instinct to get into the problem instead of waiting for someone else to solve it. From the other I learned that solving it is not enough: you have to build a way of working that makes its return less likely.

I have not become less flexible. I have become less willing to let flexibility replace the system.
