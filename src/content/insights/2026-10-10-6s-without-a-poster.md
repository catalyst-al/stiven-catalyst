A poster with six principles can hang on any wall. But the ramp does not read the poster.

The ramp shows whether the order is alive. Whether the trolleys are organised, whether the gates get closed, whether orders get mixed up, whether people move where they should. And it shows this most clearly on the hard day, not on the day an inspection comes.

5S is a method from Japan with five steps: separate what is needed from what is not, put everything in its place, clean, standardise, and sustain. 6S adds a sixth: safety. On paper it looks simple. At work it is one of the easiest things to misunderstand.

## What I have and what I do not

In last mile, in Germany, 5S was one of the operational topics we covered in our communication and in the daily stand-up. My work was directly about organising the ramp, the warehouse and the loading areas. I linked the principles of 5S to safety, to operational discipline and to KPIs such as Damage, Incomplete and Loading Time.

I do not know whether it was a formal company 6S programme with a defined structure. I do not know how it was used, if at all, in Albania or at the hotel front desk. We had operational checks and requirements to follow the ramp standards, but I do not know about a formal 5S or 6S audit programme: who ran it, how often, or how it was scored. And I do not know about floor markings, colour-coded zones or a 6S labelling system.

I will not claim that I ran a 6S programme or formal audits without documents that confirm it.

## What order looked like on the ramp

In last mile there were concrete standards:

- trolleys and loads had to be organised properly, without mixing orders;
- the drivers had to close the loading gates once loading was finished;
- chilled and frozen products had to be scanned inside their own zones;
- drivers had to keep to the waiting zones and the rules of the ramp;
- a missing bag had to be reported to the Team Leader.

And we had run into problems exactly there: mixed trolleys, loads organised wrongly, ramp rules that were not followed.

None of these is a question of appearance. Each one is linked to a number that shows up in the report the next day. A mixed trolley can become an incomplete order. A product placed badly can become damage. A disorderly ramp makes loading take longer.

## Why it is not cleaning

When someone sees 6S as cleaning, I would tell them that cleaning is only one part of it.

For me, the value of 5S and 6S is that the work becomes more organised, safer and less dependent on any one person's memory. If someone loses time looking for the right equipment, if loads get mixed or if the ramp rules are not followed, we do not just have a problem of appearance. We have an operational problem.

**An organised ramp is not just cleaner. It makes loading clearer, lets mistakes be spotted sooner and lets people work in safer conditions.**

That is why order has to be visible. When everything has its place, the thing that is out of place catches the eye. When nothing has a fixed place, nothing looks out of place.

## The sixth S

In last mile, safety was not a separate principle on the wall. It was a concrete part of the operation:

- closing the gates after loading;
- the rules for moving and standing on the ramp;
- organising loads safely;
- placing products so that damage and hazards were avoided;
- keeping to the designated zones and the hygiene rules.

Most of these are order, safety and quality at the same time. A closed gate protects people. An organised load protects the product. A respected zone protects the cold chain. So safety is not the last S added to the list. It is the reason the list exists.

## The hard day

**Order is not proved by a poster on the wall. It is proved by how the workplace runs when the day is hard.**

On a quiet day, almost any ramp looks orderly. On a day with heavy volume, people missing and delays, you see whether the standard is part of the work or something done when there is time.

I have no proof of a concrete routine I put in place myself, with a check at the end of every shift. But today I would recommend that every work area has a visible standard and someone responsible for checking it. And at the end of the shift I would check whether the equipment is in its place, whether the loads are sorted and whether open problems have been handed over to the next shift.

**6S should not be something done before an inspection. It should be part of the everyday way of working.**

## End-of-shift check for one area

If I were building it today, I would keep it short. Five points, for one area, at the end of every shift:

1. **The area has a visible standard**, and everyone knows who checks it.
2. **The equipment is in its place.**
3. **The loads are sorted**, with no mixed orders.
4. **The loading gates are closed.**
5. **Open problems have been handed over to the next shift.**

This is a recommendation I would put in place today, not a historical procedure I can confirm.

The last point links the order of the place to the order of the information. A tidy area with an open problem nobody knows about is not a tidy area.
