Any wall can carry a poster with six principles. The ramp never reads it.

What the ramp shows is whether the order is alive: whether the trolleys are organised, whether the gates get closed, whether orders get mixed up, whether people move where they should. It shows this most clearly on the hard day, not on the day an inspection comes.

5S is a method from Japan with five steps: separate what is needed from what is not, put everything in its place, clean, standardise, and sustain. 6S adds a sixth: safety. On paper it looks simple. At work it is one of the easiest things to misunderstand.

In last mile in Germany, 5S was one of the operational topics in our communication and in the daily stand-up. My work dealt directly with organising the ramp, the warehouse and the loading areas, and I linked the principles of 5S to safety, to operational discipline and to KPIs such as Damage, Incomplete and Loading Time. We had operational checks and requirements to follow the ramp standards. Whether there was also a formal company 6S programme, with its own audits, scoring, floor markings or colour-coded zones, I do not know, and I do not claim to have run one.

## What order looked like on the ramp

In last mile there were concrete standards:

- trolleys and loads had to be organised properly, without mixing orders;
- the drivers had to close the loading gates once loading was finished;
- chilled and frozen products had to be scanned inside their own zones;
- drivers had to keep to the waiting zones and the rules of the ramp;
- a missing bag had to be reported to the Team Leader.

And we had run into problems in exactly those places: mixed trolleys, loads organised wrongly, ramp rules that were not followed.

Each of these standards is tied to a number that shows up in the report the next day. A mixed trolley can become an incomplete order. A product placed badly can become damage. A disorderly ramp makes loading take longer.

## Why it is not cleaning

When someone sees 6S as cleaning, I would tell them that cleaning is only one part of it.

For me, the value of 5S and 6S is that the work becomes more organised, safer and less dependent on any one person's memory. If someone loses time looking for the right equipment, if loads get mixed or if the ramp rules are not followed, the cost shows up in the operation.

**An organised ramp is more than a cleaner ramp. It makes loading clearer, lets mistakes be spotted sooner and lets people work in safer conditions.**

That is why order has to be visible. When everything has its place, the thing that is out of place catches the eye. When nothing has a fixed place, nothing looks out of place.

## The sixth S

In last mile, safety was part of the operation itself:

- closing the gates after loading;
- the rules for moving and standing on the ramp;
- organising loads safely;
- placing products so that damage and hazards were avoided;
- keeping to the designated zones and the hygiene rules.

Most of these are order, safety and quality at the same time. A closed gate protects people. An organised load protects the product. A respected zone protects the cold chain. So safety, rather than being the last S added to the list, is the reason the list exists.

## The hard day

**Order is not proved by a poster on the wall. It is proved by how the workplace runs when the day is hard.**

On a quiet day, almost any ramp looks orderly. On a day with heavy volume, people missing and delays, you see whether the standard is part of the work or something done when there is time.

Today I would recommend that every work area has a visible standard and someone responsible for checking it, and at the end of the shift I would check whether the equipment is in its place, whether the loads are sorted and whether open problems have been handed over to the next shift. 6S should be part of the everyday way of working, not something done before an inspection.

## End-of-shift check for one area

If I were building it today, I would keep it short. Five points, for one area, at the end of every shift:

1. **The area has a visible standard**, and everyone knows who checks it.
2. **The equipment is in its place.**
3. **The loads are sorted**, with no mixed orders.
4. **The loading gates are closed.**
5. **Open problems have been handed over to the next shift.**

The last point links the order of the place to the order of the information. A tidy area with an open problem nobody knows about is not a tidy area.
