Some days in last mile, demand ran higher than the forecast. Drivers had to be called in at short notice, some arrived late, and some, still in their ramp-up phase, were not yet fully used to the SOPs. All of it landed on how the loading was organised and on the departure schedule. That is how my reports from last mile record it, in June 2025.

They were real situations of operational pressure, not necessarily holidays or a known season. What they showed me is something an ordinary day does not.

## Where the pressure landed

As Area Manager I had four Shift Leaders and eight dispatchers. In the daily stand-up we talked about the KPI results, loading problems, delays, safety and operational coordination.

The documented problems point to where attention was needed when the pressure rose:

- how trolleys and loads were organised;
- loading time and keeping to the departure schedule;
- scanning products in their designated zones;
- discipline and safety on the ramp;
- communication between drivers, dispatchers and Shift Leaders.

None of these problems is new. The same ones show up on an ordinary day. Pressure does not always create the problems. Often it makes them more visible, because on the hard day there is no time left to hide them.

## Capacity is more than a headcount

On days like that, the first question is usually: how many drivers do we have?

The sentence that has stayed with me from those reports is a different one: some drivers in ramp-up were not yet fully used to the SOPs. They were there. They were counted. They could not yet do the work the way the others did.

**Capacity is not just the number of people you have available. It is the number of people who can do the work to the standard.**

The same goes for vehicles. In August 2025 their availability became a problem: a DSP was not using its own vehicles, and the reserve capacity was already planned in. On paper there was a reserve. In practice, the reserve was already taken.

In [“Watch how I do it” is not training](/insights/watch-how-i-do-it-is-not-training/) I wrote about why a new person is not ready just because they have watched someone else do the job. The high-volume day is where that lesson costs the most.

## What the hard day reveals

When demand rises, you find out how well the operation has been prepared, how clearly responsibilities have been set and how able people are to apply the standard.

In [6S without a poster](/insights/6s-without-a-poster/) I wrote that order is proved on the hard day. The same holds for the plan. A plan that only works when everything goes as forecast describes an ordinary day.

## Before the big day

To a manager facing the first big day of the season, I would say today: do not wait for the volume to rise to find out whether the team is ready. There was operational planning of drivers, vehicles and departures back then. The five things I would recommend are advice for today:

1. **Check real capacity**, beyond the number of drivers planned.
2. **Verify that new people have been trained** for the process.
3. **Decide which standards must not be broken**, above all safety and the critical checks.
4. **Have a plan** for absences, delays and problems with vehicles.
5. **Make clear before the shift who decides** and who has to be told when the situation changes.

The third point matters most to me. On the hard day, something will be pushed back. The question is whether you decided beforehand what is never pushed back, or whether you decide it in the middle of the pressure.

**The standard is not proved on the day everything goes to plan. It is proved on the day the plan is not enough.**
