Every number in the report is made somewhere. In last mile, for me, that place was the ramp, the warehouse and the loading areas. Loading Time, Delay, Damage and Incomplete came from there and showed up in the report the next day. Gemba is the Japanese word for that place: where the work is done.

In [KPIs do not improve in Excel](/insights/kpis-do-not-improve-in-excel/) I wrote about why you have to go where the process happens before you demand a result from a table. This essay takes the next question: once you are there, what do you look at, how do you behave, and what do you do with what you see?

## What I looked at

On the ramp and in the warehouse, my attention kept going back to a few things, and the problems behind them were real:

- how the trolleys and transport boxes were organised;
- loading gates that were left open;
- discipline in parking and moving around the ramp;
- scanning, how loads were organised and whether procedures were followed;
- anything that could affect Loading Time, Delay, Damage and Incomplete.

None of these has a line of its own in the report. The report says Loading Time was high. It does not say that the trolleys were badly organised, or that movement on the ramp was disorderly. You only see that on the floor.

In the hotel, my relationship with the floor was different. Front Office and Night Audit were my daily work, so the floor was where I worked. There the risk runs the other way. You are so far inside the work that you may stop seeing the process as a process.

## Not an inspection walk

A manager can go to the floor and come back understanding nothing more. If you only go to find out who is not doing the job properly, people see it as an inspection. And a process that is being inspected behaves differently while the manager is there.

**Gemba should not be an inspection walk. It should be a way to understand the process, to listen to people and to find the obstacles the numbers do not explain.**

How people experienced my being there, I cannot say. What I believe is that a manager's presence on the floor should help people solve problems without leaving them feeling constantly watched.

## From observation to action

Seeing is only half of it. In last mile we followed the operational problems and the KPIs in the daily stand-up, and problems were communicated and followed up with the Shift Leaders and dispatchers. Whether every observation from the floor was written down and taken to the stand-up systematically, I cannot show. I have no record of a personal routine like that.

I have seen the other side, though. I recorded cases where requests for clarification about delays, damage and incomplete orders did not get an answer in time. That is where many observations get lost. Someone sees the problem, asks about it, and the question goes unanswered. The next day the problem is still there, but now it looks normal.

Being on the floor, then, is only the start. You still have to listen to people, check the facts and turn what you observe into actions.

## What I would tell a new manager

**A manager who only sees the report knows the result of the work. A manager who also goes to the floor has a chance to understand how that result is made.**

To a new manager who never leaves the office, I would say: do not try to understand the whole operation from reports alone. If Loading Time is high, go and see what happens during loading. If Incomplete goes up, follow the path of the order. And do not start by looking for someone to blame.

When you come back, write down the facts you saw. Then decide what happens with it, who takes it on and when it is checked.

## A visit to the floor

I had no Gemba routine on record: no fixed schedule or frequency, no set list of questions, no documented case where I compared a report with what I had seen myself. Today I would build a visit to the floor around five questions:

1. **Which result brought me here?** A number from the report.
2. **What did I see?** Facts first; impressions can wait.
3. **What did the people doing the work tell me?** Before I explain.
4. **Which obstacle does the number not explain?**
5. **What action follows, who takes it on and when is it checked?**

The first question takes you to the floor with a reason, and the [KPI Diagnostic](/tools/kpi-diagnostic/) helps with it: understanding what the number is telling you before you go and look. The last question brings you back with an action. Between them lies the real work, seeing and listening. After a visit, fill in the sheet below and copy it.
