When a situation needs responsibility, leadership without a title means, for me, stepping out from behind your job description without starting to do the boss's job. It depends just as much on knowing when to stop.

## Beyond the role, inside the mandate

In last mile I had an operational role, but there were moments when the work asked for more than my own task. I took responsibility for coordinating problems during the shift, following the KPIs, process discipline on the ramp, communication with the drivers, and raising the problems that kept repeating. When I saw that an incident had turned into a pattern, I tried to move the discussion from “who made the mistake?” to “what needs to change in the process?”

In the hotel it has happened differently. My role is operational, but I have gone beyond running the shift by structuring checklists, SOPs, training materials and, later, a digital system for Front Office and Night Audit.

Management's authority stayed with management. What I took responsibility for was the way I organised and documented the work I knew. The distinction matters to me: taking on more responsibility does not mean making decisions that are not yours to make.

What I can point to is what followed. I have kept being involved in coordination, operational improvements, documentation and building processes, and in the hotel I have kept working on structuring the Night Audit and the tools that make the work clearer. That tells me my contribution has been used.

## How authority is earned without a position

I do not believe authority without a title is earned by behaving as if you had a title. It is earned by being useful.

In operations, people work out very quickly whether the person in front of them only gives opinions or is there when there is a problem.

I tried to be clear, to keep panic from spreading when things went wrong, and to keep hold of a problem instead of pushing it onto someone else just to get it off my desk.

Another part was understanding the process before asking anything of people. It is much easier to ask for a standard to be respected when you understand why it exists. And when there was a problem, I tried to keep the conversation on the facts and the process, away from the person.

Authority that comes only from a position works for as long as people have to listen to you. Authority that comes from credibility works even when they are not obliged to.

## “This is not mine to decide”

I draw the line at the mandate.

I can identify a problem, gather information, propose a solution and do the part that falls within my responsibility. But if the decision touches company policy, significant costs, personnel matters, security or exceptions to procedures, or needs a manager's authorisation, that is where I stop and escalate.

I have learned that initiative and authority are two different things. Initiative says: “I will not ignore the problem.” Authority says: “I have the right to make this decision.” You can have a great deal of the first without claiming the second.

## The price

The most visible price is tiredness. Once you start to see everything as a problem that needs solving, it is very easy to take on more responsibility than is yours.

The other risk is harder to see: if you keep covering the gaps in a system, you can create the impression that the system works, because someone is always rescuing it.

Today I would draw one line more clearly, between “I am solving this now so the operation can continue” and “this problem needs a structural solution from the person who has the authority for it”. I would document more and make it clearer who owns a problem. And I would not confuse being willing to help with being obliged to carry every problem of the organisation.

Leadership is not becoming the person without whom nothing works. Leadership is helping things work even when you are not there.

## Three questions before you take on something that is not yours

Professional maturity is measured by how much responsibility you are ready to take, and also by whether you know which responsibilities are not yours to take. Today I would ask myself three questions:

1. **Am I helping because this situation needs action, or am I making a decision someone else should make?** This helps me separate initiative from overstepping the mandate.
2. **Do I have the information and the competence needed to take this on?** The fact that nobody else is acting at that moment does not automatically make me the right person to decide.
3. **Afterwards, who does the responsibility stay with?** Doing something outside your role for a moment is not always the biggest problem. The problem starts when an exception becomes normal and nobody knows any more who owns what.
