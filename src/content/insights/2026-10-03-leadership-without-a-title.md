For me, leadership without a title is not doing the boss's job without being the boss.

It is not hiding behind your job description when a situation needs responsibility. But it is just as important to know when to stop.

## Beyond the role, not beyond the mandate

In last mile I had an operational role, but in practice there were moments when the work asked for more than just doing my own task. I took responsibility for coordinating problems during the shift, following the KPIs, process discipline on the ramp, communication with the drivers, and raising the problems that kept repeating. When I saw that a problem was not just an incident but was turning into a pattern, I tried to move the discussion from "who made the mistake?" to "what needs to change in the process?".

In the hotel it has happened in a different way. My role is operational, but I have gone beyond running the shift by structuring checklists, SOPs, training materials and, later, a digital system for Front Office and Night Audit.

That does not mean I took on management's authority. I took responsibility for the way I organised and documented the work I knew.

For me this is an important distinction. Taking on more responsibility does not mean making decisions that are not yours to make.

## How authority is earned without a position

I do not believe authority without a title is earned by behaving as if you had a title. It is earned by being useful.

In operations, people work out very quickly whether the person in front of them only gives opinions, or is there when there is a problem.

I tried to be clear, not to create panic when things went wrong, and not to push a problem onto someone else just so it was no longer on my desk.

Another part was understanding the process before asking anything of people. It is much easier to ask for a standard to be respected when you understand yourself why that standard exists. And when there was a problem, I tried to talk about the fact and the process, not to attack the person.

Authority that comes only from a position works for as long as people have to listen to you. Authority that comes from credibility works even when they are not obliged to.

## "This is not mine to decide"

I draw the line at the mandate.

I can identify a problem. I can gather information. I can propose a solution. I can do the part that falls within my responsibility.

But if the decision touches company policy, significant costs, personnel matters, security, exceptions to procedures, or needs a manager's authorisation, that is where I stop and escalate.

I have learned that initiative and authority are two different things.

Initiative says: "I will not ignore the problem."

Authority says: "I have the right to make this decision."

You can have a lot of the first without claiming the second.

## What I can say about the reactions

Here I want to be careful. I do not have enough material to claim that everyone reacted in a particular way, or that I received formal recognition for every extra responsibility.

What I can say is that I have kept being involved in coordination, operational improvements, documentation and building processes. In the hotel I have kept working on structuring the Night Audit and the tools that make the work clearer. That tells me my contribution has been used.

I will not write more than that, because I do not want to claim reactions, praise or promises I do not have documented.

## The price

The most visible price is tiredness. When you start to see everything as a problem that needs solving, it is very easy to take on more responsibility than is yours.

The other risk is less visible: if you keep covering the gaps in a system, you can create the impression that the system works. Because someone is always rescuing it.

Today I would do one thing more clearly: I would separate "I am solving this now so the operation can continue" from "this problem needs a structural solution from the person who has the authority for it". I would document more. I would make the ownership of a problem clearer. And I would not confuse being willing to help with being obliged to carry every problem of the organisation.

Leadership is not becoming the person without whom nothing works. Leadership is helping things work even when you are not there.

## Three questions before you take on something that is not yours

Today I would ask myself three questions:

1. **Am I helping because this situation needs action, or am I making a decision someone else should make?** This helps me separate initiative from overstepping the mandate.
2. **Do I have the information and the competence needed to take this on?** The fact that nobody else is acting at that moment does not automatically mean I am the right person to decide.
3. **Afterwards, who does the responsibility stay with?** The biggest problem is not always doing something outside your role for a moment. The problem starts when an exception becomes normal and nobody knows any more who owns what.

Professional maturity is not measured only by how much responsibility you are ready to take. It is also measured by whether you know which responsibilities are not yours to take.
