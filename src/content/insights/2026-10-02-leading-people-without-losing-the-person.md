In last mile, telling someone to “work better” only helps once they understand what “better” means.

So the standards had to be concrete. Loading had to stay within target. Drivers had to stay in their own area rather than crowd around the Shift Leader's desk. After loading, the gate was closed. Chilled and frozen were scanned in their own areas. When a bag was missing, nobody carried on as if everything was normal: the problem was reported. Safety, cleanliness and order on the ramp were part of the standard, and damage, incomplete orders, delays and loading time were measured every day.

We also had a system of consequences for rules broken repeatedly. The standard was not a poster on the wall. It came with expectations, checks and accountability.

But something else mattered too, and it is often forgotten. A person has to know the standard before you judge them for not following it. Nobody can be held accountable for a rule that was never made clear to them.

That leads to the question that perhaps shapes the way you lead people more than any other: when something goes wrong, is it the process's error or the person's?

If a delivery came out incomplete, we looked at the process before anyone concluded that “the driver made a mistake”. Was the bag already missing in the warehouse? Had it gone into the wrong trolley? Did it have the wrong label? Were the bags mixed? Had the scanning been done properly? Had the complete route left the loading area? If the problem had started earlier in the process, it was not fair to turn it automatically into a performance problem of the driver's.

If, on the other hand, the process was in order, the standard was clear, the tools were there and the person still did not follow the procedure, then individual responsibility began.

So the question was: did the system create the possibility of the error, or did the person fail to follow a process that worked and that they knew?

The answer changes the whole conversation with that person. In the first case you fix the process. In the second you work on behaviour and accountability. Sometimes you have both at once.

The same logic applies when pressure comes from above. I tried to turn pressure about KPIs into an operational problem. What happened? Where did it happen? Which cases keep repeating? What do we check today? Who takes the action? If damage, delays or incomplete orders went up, I needed to understand the reason, and a name to blame would not have given me that.

I will not claim that I always shielded the team from management; operations are more complicated than that. What I can say is this: pressure can be fair. Humiliating people is not a management method. A standard can be very strong without treating a person as a number.

This is also how I define respect at work. Respect is not never holding someone accountable. Respect is making the standard clear to them, listening to them before you judge them, and, when the responsibility is theirs, not hiding that from them.

This way of working was hardest to hold when my own position changed.

After about five months I moved from Team Leader to Area Manager. As Team Leader I was very close to daily execution: dispatch, the drivers, the live problems, the shift's performance. As Area Manager I also had to lead the people who ran the operation: four Shift Leaders and eight dispatchers, with indirect responsibility for around 250 drivers.

I could no longer be the one who walked in and solved every problem himself. I had to ask the Shift Leader to take responsibility and the dispatcher to make the decision, so that not every problem came all the way up to me.

That is a real change in leadership, especially when you are promoted from within. Yesterday you may have solved the problem side by side with a colleague. Today you have to tell the same person, in words or by the way you work: this is now yours to solve. I am here to support you, but I will not do the job for you.

When you are promoted, you should not suddenly become a different person just to prove that you are the boss now. Nor can you carry on as if nothing had changed. You have to keep the relationship with people and, at the same time, ask of them things that yesterday were not yours to ask.
