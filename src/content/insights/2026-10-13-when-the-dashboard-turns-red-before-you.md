The red on your screen is the floor as it was. Ask how old it is before you act.

In [The system is faster than you](/insights/the-system-is-faster-than-you/) I wrote that the system decides first and the name on the shift stays yours. This essay is about the moment that happens most often: nothing has been decided yet, but a number on the screen has turned red, and everyone in the room looks at you.

Since the beginning of this year I coordinate 80+ vehicles from a distance, and I wrote about what that does to a manager in [Leading 80+ vehicles you cannot see](/insights/80-vehicles-you-cannot-see/). The dashboard is most of what I have. When a number turns red, I cannot walk to the dock to see what is behind it. I have to understand it from the screen, and the screen is faster than I am, and always slightly behind the floor.

## What I have and what I do not

I will not describe what our screens show or how fast they refresh, because I do not know the exact figures and it is internal. I do not have a measured lag between an event on the floor and the moment it appears to me. I do not have a documented case where I reacted to a red number that had already resolved itself, with the cost that followed.

What I have is the question I wrote into the card of that essay, and ask myself every day since: how old is this information? And I have the three things I watch for from a distance, which turned out to be exactly the three things a red number hides: deviations that repeat, problems left hanging, and information that arrives late.

So this essay is my analysis, not a measured result, and the scene in it is a picture that anyone who has sat in front of a dashboard will recognise.

## Four steps between the floor and the red

Picture a van that should have left the dock ten minutes ago. On the dashboard, the tour is red. What had to happen for it to be red?

Someone had to see that the van was still there, or a scan had to be missing by a certain time. Someone or something had to report it. The system had to count it against the target, at its own pace. And then your screen had to draw it. Four steps, each with its own delay. Some are seconds. Some are minutes. Some, in operations where the number comes from the next morning's report, are a whole day.

{diagram}

**A red number is not the floor. It is a report about the floor, and a report has an age.**

By the time you see the red, the van may have left. The driver may already be on the road, cursing the trolley that was missing. Or the van may still be there, and the red is the first true thing you have heard today. You cannot tell the two apart from the colour. You can only tell them apart if you know how old the red is.

## Three wrong reactions

I have seen three reactions to a red number, and from a distance I have been tempted by all three.

**Calling at once.** The number turns red, the phone is in your hand before you have thought. Often the person you call is in the middle of solving the very thing you are calling about, and now has to stop to explain it to you. You have reacted to a situation that may no longer exist, and taken a pair of hands off the problem to do it. The red was a report; you treated it as the present.

**Ignoring it.** “It is always red at nine.” Sometimes true. But a threshold everyone has learned to ignore protects nothing, and the day the red means something, nobody looks. In [A bad day or a problem?](/insights/a-bad-day-or-a-problem/) I wrote about the layer most operations skip: the one that says whether today is different from the last ten days. A red you ignore by habit is a red with no history.

**Passing it on red.** The number is forwarded one level up, red and bare, with no information. This is the third mistake of [When to solve it yourself and when to take it higher](/insights/solve-it-or-escalate-it/): escalation without information. The problem has not moved towards a solution. It has only moved to someone who now has to ask ten questions to find out what you could have found out in sixty seconds.

## The first sixty seconds

What all three have in common is that the reaction is decided while the screen is red. That is the worst moment to decide anything. The right moment is before the shift.

For every number I follow, I want to know three things before I ever see it red. How old it is when it reaches me. What I will check first: a message to the dispatcher of that zone, the scan history of the tour, a look at the tours around it. And when I pick up the phone: at once, if there is a safety or service problem that will not wait; after the check, in every other case.

Then the first sixty seconds after a red are not a decision. They are a routine. Who do I ask. What do I check. What do I know for sure, and what was only reported. One-off or repeat. And only then: step in, keep watching, or take it up, with the three facts the next level needs.

**The dashboard is faster than you at showing the past. You can be faster at the present, if you ask how old the red is.**

## The threshold, once a week

One more thing, for the end of the week rather than the shift. Count the reds that resolved themselves before you did anything. If they are the majority, the threshold is set too low, and the screen is training everyone to ignore it. If they are rare, the threshold is right, and every red deserves its sixty seconds.

That count is also the best argument you will ever have with the people who set the threshold. Not “the dashboard is wrong”, but “of forty reds this week, thirty-one had resolved themselves before I could act”.

## My red number

The card below has one row for each number you follow on a screen: what it is, how old it is when you see it, what you know for sure, whether it repeats, what you do in the first sixty seconds, and when it goes up. Fill it in before the shift. [Shift Pulse](/tools/shift-pulse/) shows the week's numbers next to each other, so a red day has its ten days of history beside it before you decide what it means.

**A red number tells you where to look. It does not tell you what is there now. The distance between the two is the age of the number, and it is yours to know.**
