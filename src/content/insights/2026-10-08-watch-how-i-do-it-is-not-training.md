“Stay with them today and watch how they do it.”

This is probably the most common way a new employee starts, and it is not bad in itself. Watching someone experienced is one of the fastest ways to see what the work really looks like.

The trouble starts when watching becomes the whole of the training. The new person then learns not only the standard but also the personal way of whoever is training them: their shortcuts, their order, the things they check twice and the things they have let slide. Tomorrow, when the new person trains someone else, they will pass on their own version.

**If the training standard is “watch how I do it”, every new employee gets a different version of the same job.**

## Three nights

At the hotel where I started as a Night Auditor, I had three nights of training, from Tuesday to Thursday.

The training combined several things: the Night Audit checklist, separate documents for different processes, colleagues who showed me in practice what they did, videos and screenshots. I took notes.

The first night had an obstacle as well. The system was down for several hours, so we could not train on the whole process; the night was more of an introduction to the work and the surroundings. I wrote about it in [The first 30 days](/insights/the-first-30-days/). Whether I was working completely on my own straight after the third night, I cannot say for certain.

What I can say with certainty is what I missed most: a single, clear path from start to finish.

The information existed, but it was scattered. The checklist told you what had to be done. Another document showed you how. A video showed one specific case. A colleague explained the rest. Each piece was useful, but connecting them was up to me.

That is where I understood the difference between **having information** and **having a training system**. Information gives you the pieces. A system gives you the order, the reason, and the point at which you know you can carry on alone.

From last mile I know there were clear operational rules for safety, loading, scanning, the dock, the cold chain and the way of working, and that retraining was required in cases where standards were broken. Whether new dispatchers, dock workers or DSP drivers went through a structured programme over several days or mainly learned next to someone, I cannot say with confidence, and I have no well-documented case of training a new employee step by step myself. So this essay rests on my experience as the person being trained, and on a view of how you can tell that someone has really learned.

## When the process lives in one person

When the process is not written down as a clear path, it lives in the person who does it every day.

The fault is not theirs. Usually they are the best person you have, which is why you chose them to train others. But even the best person has their own interpretation. They know why they leave one step for later, or why they check something a different way. The new person sees only the action; the reason stays in the other person's head.

If two new people are trained by two different colleagues, they do not learn the same job. They learn two versions of it. A few months later, where there was one standard, you have several habits that resemble each other.

That is why training cannot be separated from the standard. Without [an SOP that is actually used](/insights/a-good-sop-is-not-a-document/), training becomes copying.

## “Yes, I got it” is not proof

A person can copy very well what they saw once without necessarily understanding the process. I know someone has learned when they can carry out the process without anyone telling them every step, when they know why they are doing a check and, above all, when they know what to do when things do not follow the normal script.

My test has three parts: do it yourself, tell me why, show me what you do when an exception comes up.

If all three work, the training has started to deliver. If only the first works, the person has learned to copy, which is enough for normal days and falls short on the night when something does not go as usual.

## The first hour

In the first hour I would not try to teach someone everything. I would want them to know five things:

1. What role they have.
2. What result is expected of them.
3. Which rules are not up for discussion, above all safety and compliance.
4. Who they turn to when they do not know something.
5. What they must not decide on their own without authorisation.

These five things do not make someone capable of the job. But they make sure the first mistakes are small ones.

## The first week

During the first week the depth can come: the process from start to finish, the systems, the checklists, the normal cases, the exceptions, the handover, the KPIs, the most common mistakes, escalation. Then supervised work, until the person can do it alone.

The order I would follow is simple:

**Watch. Do it with me. You do it while I watch. Do it alone. Then explain it to me.**

That is far stronger than “stay with them today and watch how they do it”. Watching is still the first step; it has stopped being the only one. What counts in the end is how consistent and safe the result stays once the trainer has gone.

## The first-week plan for a new employee

This is the plan I would propose, written for no particular workplace. The days can be shifts, and the pace can be slower for more complex work. The order is what matters.

**Day 1: understand the role and its limits.** The five things from the first hour. Where the information is. Who is who. By the end of the day, the person should be able to say in their own words what is expected of them and what they must not decide alone.

**Day 2: watch the whole process.** Once from start to finish, checklist in hand, with someone who explains not only what they do but also why. By the end of the day, the person should be able to draw or write down the order of the steps without help.

**Day 3: do it under supervision.** The new person carries out the process; the trainer stays close and steps in only when needed. At the end of the day, both note where there was uncertainty.

**Day 4: handle an exception too.** A case that does not follow the normal script: a status that does not match, a technical problem, a request that needs to be escalated. By the end of the day, the person should know not only what they did but when they would have had to take it higher.

**Day 5: work alone and explain the process backwards.** The person works alone. At the end, they explain the process from the result back to the first step: what had to come out, what made it possible, where it could break. That is the fastest way to see whether they have understood the process or only remembered it.

After day five comes the three-part test: do it yourself, tell me why, show me what you do when an exception comes up.
