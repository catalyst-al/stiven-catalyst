“Stay with them today and watch how they do it.”

This is probably the most common way a new employee starts. It is not bad in itself. Watching someone experienced is one of the fastest ways to understand what the work really looks like.

The problem starts when that becomes the whole of the training.

Because the new person does not learn only the standard. They also learn the personal way of whoever is training them: their shortcuts, their order, the things they check twice and the things they have let slide. And tomorrow, when the new person trains someone else, they will pass on their own version.

**If the training standard is “watch how I do it”, every new employee gets a different version of the same job.**

## Three nights

At the hotel where I started as a Night Auditor, I had three nights of training, from Tuesday to Thursday.

The training was a combination of several things: the Night Audit checklist, separate documents for different processes, colleagues who showed me in practice what they did, videos and screenshots. I took notes.

The first night also had an obstacle. The system was down for several hours, so that night we could not do a normal training of the whole process. It was more of an introduction to the work and the surroundings. I wrote about that night in [The first 30 days](/insights/the-first-30-days/).

I will not say that after the third night I immediately started working completely on my own. I am not certain of that, and I do not want to write it as fact.

What I can say with certainty is what I missed most: a single, clear path from start to finish.

The information existed, but it was scattered. The checklist told you what had to be done. Another document showed you how. A video showed one specific case. A colleague explained the rest.

Each piece was useful. But the connection between them was something I had to make myself.

This is exactly where I understood the difference between **having information** and **having a training system**. Information gives you the pieces. A system gives you the order, the reason, and the point at which you know you can carry on alone.

## What I will not claim

From last mile I would like to have a full description of how dispatchers, dock workers or DSP drivers started. I do not have it documented well enough to describe it with confidence.

I know there were clear operational rules for safety, loading, scanning, the dock, the cold chain and the way of working. I know there were cases where retraining was required when standards were broken. But I do not know for certain whether new people had a structured programme over several days or learned mainly by working next to someone else.

Nor will I describe how I personally trained a new employee step by step. I do not have a case documented well enough, and I do not want to fill it in just because it would be logical in a management role.

What remains is my experience as the person being trained, and a clear view of how you know that someone has really learned.

## When the process lives in one person

When the process is not written down as a clear path, it lives in the person who does it every day.

That is not that person's fault. Usually they are the best person you have, which is why you chose them to train others. But even the best person has their own interpretation. They know why they leave one step for later, or why they check something a different way. The new person only sees the action. The reason stays in the other person's head.

If two new people are trained by two different colleagues, they do not learn the same job. They learn two versions of it. And after a few months you no longer have one standard, but several habits that resemble each other.

That is why training cannot be separated from the standard. Without [an SOP that is actually used](/insights/a-good-sop-is-not-a-document/), training becomes copying.

## “Yes, I got it” is not proof

For me, “yes, I got it” is not proof that someone has learned.

A person can copy very well what they saw once. That does not necessarily mean they understand the process.

I know someone has learned when they can carry out the process without anyone telling them every step, when they know why they are doing a check and, above all, when they know what to do when things do not follow the normal script.

My test has three parts:

**Do it yourself. Tell me why. Show me what you do when an exception comes up.**

If those three things work, the training has started to deliver. If only the first works, the person has learned to copy. That is enough for normal days. It is not enough for the night when something does not go as usual.

## The first hour

In the first hour I would not try to teach someone everything. I would want them to know five things:

1. What role they have.
2. What result is expected of them.
3. Which rules are not up for discussion, above all safety and compliance.
4. Who they turn to when they do not know something.
5. What they must not decide on their own without authorisation.

These five things do not make someone capable of the job. But they make sure the first mistakes are small ones.

## The first week

Then, during the first week, the depth can come: the process from start to finish, the systems, the checklists, the normal cases, the exceptions, the handover, the KPIs, the most common mistakes, escalation. And finally supervised work, until the person is able to do it alone.

The order I would follow is simple:

**Watch. Do it with me. You do it while I watch. Do it alone. Then explain it to me.**

To me this is far stronger than “stay with them today and watch how they do it”. Watching is still the first step. But it is no longer the only one.

## The first-week plan for a new employee

This is the plan I would propose. It is not tied to any particular workplace. The days can be shifts, and the pace can be slower for more complex work. The order is what matters.

**Day 1: understand the role and its limits.**
The five things from the first hour. Where the information is. Who is who. By the end of the day, the person should be able to say in their own words what is expected of them and what they must not decide alone.

**Day 2: watch the whole process.**
Once from start to finish, checklist in hand, with someone who explains not only what they do but also why. By the end of the day, the person should be able to draw or write down the order of the steps without help.

**Day 3: do it under supervision.**
The new person carries out the process, the trainer stays close and steps in only when needed. At the end of the day, both note where there was uncertainty.

**Day 4: handle an exception too.**
A case that does not follow the normal script: a status that does not match, a technical problem, a request that needs to be escalated. By the end of the day, the person should know not only what they did, but when they would have had to take it higher.

**Day 5: work alone and explain the process backwards.**
The person works alone. At the end, they explain the process from the result back to the first step: what had to come out, what made it possible, where it could break. That is the fastest way to see whether they have understood it or only remembered it.

And after day five, the three-part test: do it yourself, tell me why, show me what you do when an exception comes up.

Training is not measured by how well the trainer explained.

It is measured by how consistent and safe the result becomes once the trainer leaves.
