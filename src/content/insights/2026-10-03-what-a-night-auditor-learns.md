During the day, management sees the activity. At night, you see the traces the activity has left behind.

I learned this as a Night Auditor in a hotel in Germany. At night you find out whether the processes that look fine during the day really work when the moment comes to reconcile them, check them and close them.

My night falls naturally into four phases.

At the start I take the handover from the previous shift and try to understand the real state of the hotel: what has been closed, what is still open, where there are problems and which issues need attention during the night.

In the middle of the shift the deeper checking begins. That is exactly when many of the differences appear between what the system shows and what really happened during the day. Reservations, statuses, documentation, financial matters and any operational problem that could stop the day from being closed are all checked.

Then comes the closing. For me this is the part where every small problem left behind during the day can become visible. Before the day is closed, I have to be convinced that the main things are in order and that what passes into the next day is understandable.

At the end comes the handover. A night shift does not end when the system closes. It ends when the person who comes after me clearly understands what happened, what was solved and what is left to follow up.

## The day's mistakes that surface at night

Most of them are not spectacular mistakes. They are small things that, if they are not caught, create a problem later.

I have come across reservations that do not fully match what really happened, room statuses that need to be verified, documentation that was not finished, invoices or postings that need checking, and information that was not passed on properly from one shift to the next. Another category is the issues nobody forgot on purpose, but that were left hanging because there was a lot of work during the day.

The night brings these things to light because the rhythm changes. You no longer have the same flow of people in front of the reception desk, and you have the chance to see more clearly behind the process.

This work has taught me that many mistakes are not born from a lack of skill. They are born from interruptions, incomplete communication and the sentence: “We'll do it later.”

Often, “later” is the night shift.

## No-shows and payments

A reservation that exists in the system does not automatically mean that everything behind it is in order.

No-shows matter because they affect how the hotel closes the day and recognises what really happened. If they are not checked, reservations can be left with the wrong status, differences can appear in the reporting, or problems can surface only later.

The same goes for payments. The check is not only to see whether “there is money”. It is to see whether what has been recorded matches what should have been recorded.

If nobody does this check, the mistakes do not disappear. They just move from one day to the next. And the longer they move, the harder it becomes to understand where they started.

## Closing the day is not a list

Closing the day has taught me that a process should not be closed just because “it is time to close it”. It should be closed when the things that depend on it are really ready.

In the first weeks, naturally, my attention was very much on the list: which step comes after which, what has to be opened, what has to be checked and in what order I have to go.

Today I see it differently. I do not only think: “Did I do this step?” I think: “Is what this step is meant to check really in order?”

That is a big change. A checklist can be completed mechanically. But a good Night Audit requires you to understand how the processes depend on each other. If something earlier has not been closed properly, there is no point in rushing the process just so the list looks finished.

This has also made me more careful with the handover. If something stays open, it has to be clear why it stayed open and who has to take it further.

## When you are the main person responsible at night

The night forces you to make decisions. During the day there are more departments, more managers and more people you can ask. At night many situations come to you first.

That does not mean you have to do everything yourself.

For me the line is fairly clear. If it is an operational problem that falls within my role, I analyse it and try to solve it. If it is a situation that needs a decision outside my authority, carries financial risk, touches security, could have serious consequences or needs an approval that is not mine to give, then I escalate it.

I have learned that responsibility is not making every decision. Responsibility is knowing which decision you can make yourself and which one you should not make.

This matters especially at night, because the temptation to say “I'm alone, I have to solve it myself” is strong. Not always. Sometimes the most professional decision is precisely not to cross the limit of your authority.

## Five minutes with the day managers

If I had five minutes with the day managers, I would tell them not to see the night report only as proof that the hotel got through another night. That report shows much more.

The five things I would look at every morning:

1. **What was left unresolved.** Not only what happened, but what still needs action.
2. **Where there were differences between the system and reality.** Reservations, statuses, documentation or financial matters that needed intervention.
3. **Which problems keep repeating.** A mistake once can be chance. The same mistake several times is a process.
4. **What affected the guest or the operation.** Because that is where you see whether an internal problem is starting to become a customer problem.
5. **What the next shift needs to know before the work starts.** A good report should not only tell the past. It should make the day that is starting safer.

This holds for every business, not only for a hotel. And sometimes, what a business hides from itself during the day shows up very clearly at night.
