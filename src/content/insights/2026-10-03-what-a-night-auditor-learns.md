During the day, management sees the activity. At night you see the traces it has left behind.

I learned this as a Night Auditor in a hotel in Germany. At night you find out whether the processes that look fine during the day actually work when the moment comes to reconcile them, check them and close them.

My night falls naturally into four phases.

At the start I take the handover from the previous shift and try to understand the real state of the hotel: what has been closed, what is still open, where there are problems and which issues need attention during the night.

In the middle of the shift the deeper checking begins, and this is when many of the differences appear between what the system shows and what really happened during the day. Reservations, statuses, documentation, financial matters and any operational problem that could stop the day from being closed are all checked.

Then comes the closing, which for me is the part where every small problem left behind during the day can become visible. Before the day is closed, I have to be convinced that the main things are in order and that whatever passes into the next day can be understood.

At the end comes the handover. A night shift does not end when the system closes. It ends when the person who comes after me clearly understands what happened, what was solved and what is left to follow up.

## The day's mistakes that surface at night

Most of them are small, unspectacular things that create a problem later if nobody catches them.

I have come across reservations that do not fully match what really happened and room statuses that need to be verified. There is documentation that was not finished, there are invoices or postings that need checking, and there is information that was not passed on properly from one shift to the next. Then there are the issues nobody forgot on purpose, left hanging because there was a lot of work during the day.

The night brings these things to light because the rhythm changes. The flow of people at the reception desk thins out, and you can see more clearly behind the process.

This work has taught me that many mistakes have little to do with skill: they come from interruptions, incomplete communication and the sentence “We'll do it later.” Often, “later” is the night shift.

## No-shows and payments

A reservation that exists in the system does not automatically mean that everything behind it is in order.

No-shows matter because they affect how the hotel closes the day and recognises what really happened. If they are not checked, reservations can be left with the wrong status, differences can appear in the reporting, or problems can surface only later.

The same goes for payments. The check is about more than whether “there is money”: it asks whether what has been recorded matches what should have been recorded.

If nobody does this check, the mistakes move on from one day to the next. And the longer they travel, the harder it becomes to see where they started.

## Closing the day is more than a list

Closing the day has taught me to close a process when the things that depend on it are ready, and not just because “it is time to close it”.

In the first weeks my attention was, naturally, on the list: which step comes after which, what has to be opened, what has to be checked and in what order.

Today I see it differently. After “Did I do this step?” comes a second question: “Is what this step is meant to check actually in order?”

That is a big change. A checklist can be completed mechanically, but a good Night Audit requires you to understand how the processes depend on each other. If something earlier has not been closed properly, there is no point in rushing the process just so the list looks finished.

This has also made me more careful with the handover. If something stays open, it has to be clear why, and who has to take it further.

## When you are the main person responsible at night

The night forces you to make decisions. During the day there are more departments, more managers and more people you can ask. At night many situations come to you first. That does not mean you have to do everything yourself.

For me the line is fairly clear. If it is an operational problem that falls within my role, I analyse it and try to solve it. If a situation needs a decision outside my authority, carries financial risk, touches security, could have serious consequences or needs an approval that is not mine to give, I escalate it.

I have learned that responsibility is not making every decision. Responsibility is knowing which decision you can make yourself and which one you should not make.

This matters especially at night, when the temptation to say “I'm alone, I have to solve it myself” is strong. That is not always true, and sometimes the most professional decision is precisely not to cross the limit of your authority.

## Five minutes with the day managers

If I had five minutes with the day managers, I would ask them to read the night report for what it shows, which is much more than proof that the hotel got through another night.

The five things I would look at every morning:

1. **What was left unresolved.** Not only what happened, but what still needs action.
2. **Where there were differences between the system and reality.** Reservations, statuses, documentation or financial matters that needed intervention.
3. **Which problems keep repeating.** A mistake once can be chance. The same mistake several times is a process.
4. **What affected the guest or the operation.** That is where you see whether an internal problem is starting to become a customer problem.
5. **What the next shift needs to know before the work starts.** A good report should not only describe the past; it should make the day that is starting safer.

This holds for every business, not only for a hotel. And sometimes what a business hides from itself during the day shows up very clearly at night.
