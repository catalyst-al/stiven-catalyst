In Albania, my last role was Cluster Manager: I ran the operations of a flagship store in Tirana and coordinated part of the store network. My first job in Germany was delivering parcels.

The series “Ten years close to the work” starts with that contrast because nothing I write afterwards makes sense without it: how I think when an operational problem lands in front of me, how I lead people, and why I see processes from both sides.

I helped build the flagship store from zero to opening day, coordinating architects, carpenters, painters and the fit-out crews. Then came sales, the service standard, the customer experience, the operational organisation and the team: eight sales staff, four cashiers and one assistant. We built the company's first online sales website and its first e-commerce team. In the same period the network was restructured: two underperforming stores closed, and the business concentrated more on the flagship.

Before that, as General Sales Manager at a distributor of household appliances, I had led the wholesale business: a network of 61 active resellers, and a structure that took in the warehouse, B2B sales, accounting, the technical team and customer service. By the time I left Albania, I was used to making decisions, leading people and answering for business results.

Then came Germany. From November 2021 to June 2022 I worked for a last-mile delivery company, first behind the wheel as a parcel courier, later in dispatch. The hotel came next: steward from July 2022 to April 2023, then, from May 2023, night auditor at a hotel in Frankfurt. Between December 2023 and October 2024 I went back to delivering parcels alongside the night audit, and from October 2024 to January 2025 I worked in care, as a care assistant (Pflegehelfer). In January 2025 I returned to last mile as Team Leader On Road; in June 2025 I became Area Manager.

I have no reason to hide the jobs as a parcel courier, a steward or a care assistant. Without them, my road back into management makes no sense.

In Germany I had to take the van, drive the route, keep to the times and the process, and do the job like any other employee. My old title no longer counted. That was the real zero: my experience had not disappeared, but nobody was obliged to recognise it on the strength of my past.

Some things helped straight away, because they do not depend on a title: operational discipline, the relationship with the customer, the ability to understand a process and see where the waste is, working under pressure, and a readiness to take responsibility. I had already moved from retail to wholesale, and from running a store to the warehouse, B2B sales, e-commerce and leading teams. That helped me understand new industries quickly.

One thing I had to leave behind was the idea that experience automatically guarantees the same position in a new place. I could not say, “I was a manager, so I should be a manager.” I had to learn the system from the inside, from the operational level up, and prove myself again.

The language was no different. When I arrived, German was not my working language; I had to build it while I worked. In 2025 I took the Deutsch-Test für Zuwanderer at level B1: 45 out of 45 in listening and reading, 15 out of 20 in writing, 97 out of 100 in speaking. I did not wait for my German to be perfect before moving forward professionally. I worked, communicated and learned at the same time.

These years gave me more than the language. They showed me the work from the side of the person who has to carry out the process.

As a parcel courier you understand time, the route and pressure differently, and you know what it means when a process does not work or a decision is taken by someone who is not on the ground. As a steward you see the hotel from another level entirely: physical work, rhythm, cooperation between departments, and how much the guest's experience depends on people the guest may never see. As a night auditor I saw what is left of a working day after everyone else has gone: the errors, the payments, the reports, the complaints, the no-shows, the checks and the handover.

When I returned to leadership in last mile, I led people with more than theory behind me: I had stood on the other side of the process myself. That experience sits behind every essay in this series.

If someone who has been a boss asked me how to start again from zero, I would not pretend it is easy, but I would tell them not to confuse losing the title with losing their professional value. A title can disappear in a single day, when you change country, company or industry. What you have learned does not.

There is one condition. Do not walk into the new job expecting others to respect what you used to be. Start showing what you can do where you are. I went from leading people and businesses in Albania to delivering parcels and working as a steward in Germany, then to night audit, to Team Leader and to Area Manager. If I had thought some jobs were beneath me, I might never have learned what I needed to rise again.

Do not cling to the chair you used to have. Take with you what you learned while sitting in it.
