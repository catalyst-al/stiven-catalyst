In the calendar, everything had its place.

The morning meeting. A project deadline. A call with the manager. A report to finish. An important meeting for the professional future.

Somewhere among them, a small reminder had also been written.

“Be home on time.”

No hour had been set by the management. It needed no confirmation by email. It did not affect the performance indicators.

And, for exactly that reason, it was perhaps the easiest commitment to move.

A meeting ran longer. A problem called for intervention. A task could not wait until tomorrow.

The reminder was pushed back.

Not because it was unimportant.

But because it seemed it could wait.

And so begins one of the quietest contradictions of modern life.

We learn to respect with precision the time we have promised to others, while we treat the time we have promised to our own people as the last reserve from which we can take whenever we need.

Then we are surprised when the years pass and we find that some of the most important things never had a final deadline.

***

I have no intention of condemning ambition.

Ambition is one of the forces that push a person beyond the conditions he finds himself in. It can drive him to learn, to take responsibility, to build something of his own and to seek a better life.

There are people who work hard because they love the profession.

There are others who work hard because they do not have the luxury of doing otherwise.

And there are people who combine both reasons.

We must be careful when we speak of the balance between work and life, because not everyone starts this discussion from the same point.

It is easy to talk about personal limits when income covers needs, savings are safe and a professional opportunity turned down does not threaten the family's stability.

It is far harder when the pay has to cover rent, obligations and a future that has not yet taken shape.

For a person who has started life over in another country, work may represent far more than a career.

It may be security.

It may be dignity.

It may be the road to a stability that once seemed out of reach.

And for a parent who wants to give a child better chances, long hours of work may be a form of sacrifice born of love, not of a thirst for status.

That is why I am not convinced by the easy advice that tells people to work less and enjoy life.

As if everyone had the same chance to choose.

As if obligations disappeared just because we have realised that time is precious.

But there is a question that deserves to be asked even when sacrifice is necessary.

How long must it go on?

And who decides when the moment has come to live a part of the life we are trying to build?

***

A career has a great advantage over personal life.

It knows how to show progress.

It has titles.

It has salaries.

It has targets met.

It has new responsibilities, finished projects and positions you can put on a professional document.

You can see where you were five years ago and where you stand today.

You can compare the road you have travelled with the chances you have had.

You can feel a legitimate pride in the difficulties you have overcome.

Personal life is not always so clear.

There is no certificate for an evening spent with the family.

There is no promotion for the patience you showed to someone who needed to talk.

There is no performance indicator for the fact that you were present at an ordinary moment that may have had extraordinary importance for someone else.

These things are not collected in a CV.

They do not necessarily help you win an interview.

You cannot present them at an annual meeting as proof of professional development.

But the lack of a measuring system does not make them less important.

It only makes them easier to neglect.

And a person who is used to valuing life through visible achievements may begin, without realising it, to give priority to everything that can be proved in front of others.

A project has a result.

A promotion has a date.

A better salary has a sum.

While an afternoon lost with your own people does not show up as a loss in any system.

Yet it is gone.

***

There is a word we often use to reconcile ourselves with the lack of time.

Later.

When this project is over, I'll be freer.

When I take the next position, I'll have more control over my schedule.

When we have financial security, we'll travel.

When we settle down, we'll spend more time together.

When this period passes, I'll start taking more care of myself.

And perhaps all these promises are made sincerely.

The problem is that professional life has an extraordinary ability to produce another phase every time.

After a project comes a bigger project.

After a promotion come new responsibilities.

After a target is reached, another appears that until yesterday did not seem essential.

Demands grow along with opportunities.

And the limit that once seemed enough begins to move.

A person may spend years believing he is only one step away from the moment when he will have time for life.

But that step keeps moving.

It is not that work never ends.

It is that ambition, without a clear sense of what it is seeking, can make every achievement look like the start of a new obligation.

And here we must stop at a question we rarely ask ourselves.

What would be enough?

Not what would be possible.

Not how high we can climb.

But which level of security, responsibility and success would let us say we no longer need to always sacrifice the present for a better future.

Without an answer, we risk never arriving at the place we thought we were going.

Even when the career goes brilliantly.

***

But there is another truth that must not be hidden.

Work can give us something that personal life does not always give with the same clarity.

A sense of competence.

At work we may have a defined role. We know what is expected of us. We can take decisions, solve problems and see results.

In personal life, things are less predictable.

Relationships ask for patience without guarantees.

Parenting has no manual that works in every situation.

Hard conversations are not always solved with an action plan.

And the people we love do not necessarily value us by how well we have handled a problem.

They may need something simpler.

To be there.

To listen.

To accept that we do not always have answers.

For a person who has built his identity around the ability to solve problems, this may be harder than it looks.

At work, you can be the manager who takes decisions.

At home, you may be simply the person someone needs to listen to.

And sometimes, without realising it, we seek shelter in the place where we feel most competent.

Work gives us problems that can be solved.

Life asks us to face things we cannot control as well.

This does not mean that everyone who works hard is avoiding personal life.

That would be an unjust judgement.

But it is a possibility worth examining honestly.

Because a moment may come when work is no longer only the means of building the life we want.

It may become the place where we feel safest from the questions that life puts to us.

***

There is a common misunderstanding in discussions of balance.

As if work and personal life must always be divided into equal parts.

Eight hours of work.

Eight hours of rest.

Eight hours for everything else.

Life does not work with such clean symmetry.

There are professions with night shifts.

There are periods when professional responsibilities are greater.

There are phases when a family needs more financial support than free time.

There are also people who find an important part of the meaning of life in the very profession they practise.

There is no single formula that can be imposed on everyone.

Indeed, sometimes a temporary sacrifice is the most responsible choice.

But the word *temporary* must mean something.

If an extraordinary period lasts for years, it has ceased to be extraordinary.

It has become our way of life.

And if the people who bear the consequences of this choice have never been part of the conversation, then we have reason to ask how fair it is.

Because professional decisions may be taken by one individual.

But their consequences are not always borne by him alone.

A husband or wife may carry more responsibility at home.

A child may grow used to a parent's absence.

Friendships may fade.

Health may call for attention that is constantly put off.

And all of this can happen without any great conflict, without an ultimatum and without a dramatic moment.

Sometimes relationships are not broken by a single decision.

They are weakened by hundreds of small absences, each of which, taken alone, looked justifiable.

***

But presence too needs to be understood.

To be physically at home does not necessarily mean to be present.

You can sit in the same room as your people and still have your mind on an email that has had no answer.

You can spend an afternoon with your child while your attention keeps going back to the phone.

You can take some days off and go on living under the pressure of a job you cannot leave behind even temporarily.

Here the problem is no longer only the number of hours.

It is the ability to be present in the hours we have.

Of course, even this cannot be demanded perfectly.

A person gets tired.

He has worries.

There are days when he cannot give all the attention he wants.

And a parent who comes home exhausted from work must not be treated as having failed morally because he does not have endless energy.

But there is a difference between a tiredness we cannot always avoid and the habit of leaving personal life constantly on hold.

The first calls for understanding.

The second calls for honesty with ourselves.

Because sometimes we do not lack time in the absolute sense.

We have lost the ability to decide which part of our time must not always be negotiable.

***

This is also a responsibility of management, not only of the individual.

An organisation cannot go on building results on the unlimited availability of its employees and then talk to them about the importance of personal life.

It cannot demand permanent availability, reward the people who never disconnect from work and at the same time claim to support balance.

The real message of an organisation is not always found in the human resources policies.

It is found in what it values in practice.

Who is promoted?

Who is regarded as dedicated?

Is the employee who finishes tasks within working hours as respected as the one who always stays longer?

Is work planned so that ordinary absences do not create a crisis?

Do people have the chance to disconnect during their time off, except where there are clear and lawful on-call duties?

And do managers have the courage to admit that part of the excess load comes from the way work has been organised, not from a lack of dedication in the staff?

These are not sentimental questions.

They are questions about the sustainability of an organisation.

Because a company that relies constantly on personal sacrifice may get good results for a period.

But it must not confuse that period with a long-term model of management.

People are not resources that return automatically to their initial state after every weekend.

They have a life beyond work.

And that life affects their ability to think, to decide and to stay healthy.

A manager who understands this is not lowering professional standards.

He is accepting the human limits on which every serious standard must be built.

***

But I want to return to the individual.

To the person who goes on working, learning and asking something more of himself.

I do not think the solution is to give up ambition.

A life without aims does not automatically become a better one.

And it is not fair to ask someone to shrink his dreams just because the road to them takes effort.

The problem does not lie in the wish to move forward.

It lies in the risk of no longer asking why we want to move forward.

To build what?

To protect whom?

To win what kind of freedom?

And which parts of life are we not ready to lose along the way?

These questions do not shrink our aims.

They give them direction.

Because a person can have a brilliant career and a rich personal life.

He can be ambitious and present.

He can work hard and know his own limits.

But this does not always happen by itself.

It takes choices.

Sometimes it takes turning down an opportunity that looks wonderful on paper but would have a personal cost you do not wish to pay.

Sometimes it takes accepting a hard opportunity, because you know the sacrifice has a clear purpose and a defined limit.

And sometimes it takes accepting that what you wanted ten years ago is not necessarily what you want today.

This is not a loss of ambition.

It is the ability to develop even in the way you understand success.

***

There is a change that happens in a person's life, often without any warning.

At one age, success may mean leaving the place where you are.

Taking a better position.

Testing your ability.

Winning independence.

Later, success may take other forms.

To have the chance to choose.

Not to be always under the pressure of financial obligation.

To have time for the people you love.

To keep your health.

To work in an environment where you do not have to defend your dignity every day.

These changes are not the same for everyone.

And there is no reason why a person should not go on aiming at high positions, great responsibilities or professional achievements even when his life changes.

But he must be free to revise his definitions of success.

Otherwise he may go on pursuing an ambition that once had meaning but that now keeps him moving mainly out of habit.

A person can change a great deal over the years.

It is strange to expect his definition of a good life to stay always the same.

***

And there is something that not even the most successful career can change.

Time does not wait for us to finish our plans.

A child does not stay the same age until the parent reaches professional stability.

A parent does not always keep the same strength until we have the chance to spend more time with them.

A relationship cannot be preserved indefinitely by the promise alone that tomorrow we will have more room.

This does not mean every absence is irreparable.

People can build strong relationships even under hard working conditions.

They can recover closeness, change habits and create new memories.

But they cannot turn time back.

A child may have his parent closer after a few years.

But he will no longer be the child of the same age.

And this is not an accusation against the parent who worked to provide for the family.

It is a reminder of the limit that every sacrifice has.

Some things can be put off.

Others change while we wait.

***

In the end, I do not believe there is a life in which all the choices are perfect.

Every road has a price.

The person who gives more time to his career may lose some personal opportunities.

The one who decides to give priority to the family in a certain period may give up some professional opportunities.

One may choose security.

Another risk.

One may build a happy life through a hard profession.

Another may realise he needs a completely different rhythm.

I am not interested in dividing these choices into right and wrong by a universal formula.

I am interested in whether the person understands what he is choosing.

Whether he is honest with himself and with the people affected by his decisions.

Whether the sacrifice has a purpose and a limit.

And whether, from time to time, he has the courage to review the road before habit turns it into fate.

Because not every professional opportunity offered to us is one we must accept.

And not everything we can achieve automatically deserves all the time it demands.

This is perhaps one of the hardest forms of maturity.

To understand that the ability to achieve something is not always a sufficient reason to pursue it.

***

One day, perhaps, each of us will look back at the road we have travelled.

He will remember the hard decisions.

The jobs he did.

The responsibilities he carried.

The people who helped him and those he helped himself.

He may have reason to feel proud.

And he should feel proud.

Because building a career takes effort, discipline and sacrifices that must not be devalued.

But I hope that, alongside the professional achievements, there is another story to remember.

Not a perfect story.

Not a life without absences, without mistakes and without hard periods.

But a life in which the people were not only the reason he worked.

They were also part of what he lived.

Where success was not always something expected to happen later.

Where there were moments that did not need to produce any result to have value.

And where the person did not spend all his time preparing for a future, without ever finding room for the present.

***

At the end of the week, the calendar may be full again.

There will be meetings.

Tasks.

Deadlines.

Opportunities we do not want to lose.

And responsibilities we cannot avoid.

That is how a large part of adult life is.

But among them there may also be another commitment.

An evening.

A conversation.

A visit.

A walk for no particular reason.

A moment that does not need to be productive.

We do not have to romanticise it.

It does not have to be unforgettable.

It is enough not to treat it automatically as the first thing that can be sacrificed.

Because sometimes it is precisely the moments that do not look extraordinary that, years later, we wish we had had more of.

A career has the right to ask for dedication.

But it must not be given, without discussion, the right to define the value of our whole life.

**There is no shame in wanting to achieve a lot. Nor is there shame in admitting that, to achieve something, you must temporarily sacrifice something else.**

But it would be tragic to achieve everything you once wanted and only then realise you had never asked what you did not want to lose.

Because a career can be built over years.

It can change direction.

It can start again from scratch.

It can take a form we cannot imagine today.

Life, meanwhile, goes on.

With its people.

With the ages that change.

With the opportunities that come and go.

With the ordinary things that gain meaning only when we no longer have them.

And perhaps the question we should ask ourselves from time to time is not how far we have come professionally.

It is whether the road we have chosen still leads towards the life we began working for.

**Because the saddest success is not the one we failed to build.**

**It is the one we built with so much devotion that when we finally raised our heads to enjoy it, we found that a part of the life it was meant to fill had passed without us.**
