At six in the morning, the problem was still there.

The night shift had spent hours correcting mistakes that should never have happened. A procedure had not worked as planned. Some customers had waited longer than usual. Part of the work had been finished only thanks to individual interventions, not because the system had worked well.

The first report said:

“Repeated problems in the process. Intervention needed.”

The head of department read it. The problem was real, but he did not want the report to give the impression that the situation had gone out of control. He changed the wording:

“Operational difficulties identified and managed.”

The manager at the next level shortened it even more. After all, the work had been completed and the customers had gone.

“Operational challenges were handled successfully.”

By the time the report reached the executive floor, the sentence had taken its final form:

“Operations completed normally.”

None of the people who had touched the document considered himself a liar.

Each had changed only a few words.

But upstairs, the director was reading the story of a day that had never existed.

***

In modern organisations, information is constantly travelling.

From the employee to the shift lead. From the shift lead to the manager. From the manager to the executive floor. From the executive floor to those who take strategic decisions.

In theory, this is a necessary mechanism. You cannot expect a leader to be physically present in every department, at every hour and for every problem.

He needs reports, indicators, analyses and people who can tell the important from the unimportant.

But there is a great difference between summarising information and changing its meaning.

A good report reduces the amount of information without distorting reality.

A bad report reduces the discomfort that reality might cause its reader.

And when this practice repeats, the organisation starts to develop a strange skill: to look ever healthier on paper while its problems grow ever harder to solve.

On the ground, people know one company.

On the executive floor, they read about another.

***

Yet this distortion rarely begins with an open order to hide the truth.

More often it begins with fear of how the truth will be received.

A manager knows the director does not like unpleasant surprises. A head of department has learned that negative reports come with questions, criticism and doubts about his ability. An employee has seen that the colleague who raised a serious concern ended up being treated as if he were himself the source of the problem.

Nobody needs to write a rule banning bad news.

It is enough for people to understand what happens to the one who brings it.

Then self-censorship begins.

First a detail judged too negative is dropped. Later a direct word is replaced with a more diplomatic phrase. A repeated mistake becomes “a situation being monitored”. A serious staff shortage is described as “resource optimisation”. A process that survives only thanks to the extraordinary efforts of its employees is presented as proof of the efficiency of the structure.

The information keeps circulating.

Only the truth begins to get lost on the way.

And perhaps the most disturbing aspect is that this process can unfold without any deliberate agreement to deceive.

Everyone is trying to protect something.

One his reputation. Another his position. Another his relationship with his superior. Someone is trying to avoid conflict. Someone thinks the problem will solve itself and that it is not worth making a fuss.

In the end, all these individual efforts at self-protection produce a collective lie.

***

But responsibility does not lie only with those who send the information.

Often it begins in the office where the information is meant to arrive.

There are leaders who declare constantly that they want transparency.

“I want to know everything. Don't hide any problem from me.”

Admirable words.

Then comes the day when someone takes them seriously.

A manager reports that the target will not be met. Another shows that the procedure approved by the board has had unforeseen consequences. A head of department explains that a cost-saving decision is damaging the quality of service.

And the leader reacts.

Not necessarily with shouting. Sometimes contempt is enough. An ironic remark. A question that treats the reporting of a problem as proof of incompetence. A public doubt about the competence of the person who brought the news.

People watch.

They learn not only from what the leader says he wants.

They learn from what he rewards and what he punishes.

If the leader rewards the man who brings polished results and humiliates the one who reports a real failure, the organisation will begin to adapt.

Not necessarily by improving the results.

By improving the way they are presented.

This creates a quiet form of informational corruption, where people do not sell decisions for money, but trade the truth for the security of their position.

And the leader, who believes he is exercising firm control, may be the very man who created the conditions in which he no longer receives reliable information.

***

There is a heavy paradox in the relationship between power and information.

The higher a person climbs in the hierarchy, the more authority he gains to change reality.

But at the same time he may lose the chance to see it without filters.

The employee on the ground knows the mistake because he has to correct it. He knows the staff shortage because he has to cover the work. He understands the weakness of the procedure because he meets its consequences every day.

The senior leader knows the same situation through a table, a presentation or an explanation prepared by someone else.

This does not automatically make the employee more accurate and the leader less capable. The person on the ground may see only one part of the problem. He may not know the financial constraints, the legal obligations or the consequences a decision has in other departments.

That is exactly why an honest flow of information is needed.

Experience from the ground must meet the overall view of leadership.

But when information is filtered to protect the hierarchy, this link is cut.

The leader keeps taking decisions.

Only more and more of them rest on a reality selected by others.

And authority, when cut off from knowledge of reality, can become very dangerous even in the hands of a man with good intentions.

***

Sometimes the deception is not in the words of the report.

It is in what has been chosen to measure.

A company may report that all tasks were completed on time.

But how many were completed correctly?

It may show that the number of complaints has fallen.

But has the service improved, or have customers lost faith that their complaint will change anything?

It may boast of a lower cost for an operation.

But has anything really been saved, or has part of the work simply been moved to another department?

These questions do not devalue performance indicators.

On the contrary, they are the reason why indicators must be built and interpreted with care.

A number can be mathematically accurate and still lead to a wrong conclusion, if it is used out of context.

This happens especially when the organisation becomes more interested in how the indicator looks than in the phenomenon it is meant to represent.

Then people learn to improve what is seen.

Not necessarily what matters.

The report may be green.

The problem may still be alive.

***

But it would be just as wrong to demand that every suspicion, grievance or individual interpretation be treated as the final truth.

Transparency also needs discipline.

A serious leader must tell facts from perceptions, isolated cases from systematic problems, and well-founded warnings from assumptions.

He must not declare every concern a crisis.

He must create the conditions in which concerns can be examined without fear.

An honest report may say that a process failed, that the cause has not yet been identified and that data is being gathered to understand it.

This is not a sign of weakness.

It is the difference between an organisation that wants to know and an organisation that wants to look as if it knows.

Because admitting uncertainty is also information.

And sometimes it is far more valuable than a certainty invented to calm the leaders.

***

There is a question every manager should ask himself from time to time:

When was the last time someone told me something I did not want to hear?

Not trivial criticism, not an ordinary complaint, but a truth that called into question a decision of mine, a belief of mine or the way I had understood the situation.

If the answer is “I don't remember”, that is not necessarily proof that everything is going well.

It may be a sign that people have stopped trying.

A leader does not build trust by repeating that his office door is always open.

He builds it by how he behaves when someone comes through that door with a problem he himself helped create.

Does he listen before defending himself?

Does he ask for facts, or start looking for culprits?

Does he value the person who reports a risk, even when the report is imperfect?

Is he ready to admit that the decision he took may have been wrong?

These are more important tests than public declarations about a culture of transparency.

Because people do not need a leader who adores criticism.

They need a leader who is able to withstand it.

***

There is a reason why organisations that hide their problems are often surprised when they explode.

From the outside, everything looked normal.

Reports arrived on time. Objectives were presented with care. Meetings ended with positive conclusions. Nobody had raised any serious alarm.

Then an important customer leaves. A mistake becomes public. A good professional resigns. A process fails at a critical moment.

The board asks:

“How is it possible that nobody informed us?”

Sometimes the answer is that somebody had.

Not perfectly. Not always with full documentation. Perhaps only with a note, a warning or a request for help.

But the information had begun its journey.

And on the way it had lost its importance, its urgency or its meaning.

At each level it had become a little more acceptable.

Until it reached the top as a piece of news that no longer called for any action.

And that is perhaps the costliest way to lose control of an organisation: by going on believing that you have it.

***

The solution is not for the director to suspect every report and personally check every detail.

An organisation that works only under the constant supervision of its leader has a different problem.

The solution is to build a culture in which unpleasant information is not treated as a hostile act.

Where an employee can report a mistake without being humiliated.

Where a manager can admit a poor result without feeling the need to invent a success story.

Where the board asks not only for achievements, but also for an explanation of what did not work.

Where a reported problem is not closed simply because it was logged, but is followed through to a verifiable conclusion.

This demands something else too, far harder than creating a procedure.

Intellectual humility.

The ability to accept that a higher position in the hierarchy does not guarantee the most accurate understanding of reality.

A leader may have more authority than everyone in a department.

But authority does not automatically give him the knowledge they have gained by working every day with problems he knows only from reports.

And a wise organisation does not try to hide this difference.

It tries to use it.

***

In the end, the truth does not need a big office.

It can be found in an error log, in a tiring shift, in a complaint that keeps coming back, in a procedure that employees are forced to bypass to get the work done.

It can come from a person with no title, no authority and no ability to express the problem in the polished language of management.

But that does not make it any less important.

Because reality does not change with the rank of the one who describes it.

A problem stays a problem even when it is reported as a challenge.

A failure stays a failure even when it is presented as an opportunity for improvement.

And an organisation does not grow stronger by learning to replace unpleasant words with acceptable phrases.

It grows stronger when it manages to hear what it does not like, to examine it honestly and to act on what is revealed.

That is why a leader who wants to know the true state of his organisation must begin with a question rarely found in performance reports:

**What are my people afraid to tell me?**

If he manages to get an honest answer, he may discover that some of the most serious problems were never invisible.

They were only unwanted.

Because the truth is not always stopped by people who want to lie.

Sometimes it is stopped by people who have learned that it is safer to keep quiet.

And at the head of an organisation there may stand a leader convinced that he controls everything, surrounded by reports accurate in form but emptied of what matters most.

He has the numbers.

He has the presentations.

He has the approval of all the managers.

Only reality is missing.

**Because power can force people to give you answers. But it cannot force them to tell you the truth, if you have taught them to fear it.**

And when the truth no longer dares to climb to the top floor, it is not the information that has failed to reach leadership.

It is leadership that has lost the right to hear it.
