For years, in the offices of Albanian directors and owners, one sentence has gone round that was used as a hammer against every request for dignity, a pay rise or better conditions: "Let them leave, there are as many people as you like waiting at the door to work."

It was the ready answer of an arrogance that knew no limits. It was the philosophy of a market in which a person was seen as a cheap spare part, thrown away as soon as it was worn out, to be replaced by the next in line. And for a long time poverty and the lack of alternatives kept this illusion alive. Managers learned to think that value lay only in the walls of their business, and not in the hands and minds of those who kept that business open.

But today, when we open the door where there was once thought to be a queue, we see only empty corridors. The queue no longer exists. And with it, the most dangerous lie of Albanian management has fallen too.

**Let me be direct. You can replace a name on the payroll within a day, but you can never replace the experience, the memory of the process and the trust that leave together with a professional.**

When a long-serving employee puts the keys on the table and leaves, they do not take only their wage with them. They take hundreds of small, invisible solutions that are written in no rulebook. They take the calm of a hard shift, the knowledge of every customer, the ability to prevent a mistake before it turns into a cost. They take with them what holds every real operation up: personal responsibility.

Weak managers do not see this loss, because in their spreadsheets two people are worth the same, as long as the wage at the end of the row is the same or lower. They think that by hiring someone without experience and paying them less, they have struck a clever bargain.

But the reality of operations does not forgive. The cost of the one who left does not show on the first day. It shows in the second week, when orders start to run late. It shows in the first month, when mistakes multiply, goods get damaged and customers begin to complain. And worst of all, it shows in the rest of the team left behind. When you push out a capable person with contempt, you have told everyone else that effort and loyalty in that company are worth nothing.

I have seen up close how businesses led by this cynicism wither. First the best leave, those with the confidence and the knowledge to find other doors, inside or outside the country. Then those leave who are tired of carrying on their backs the weight of those who left first. In the end only two kinds of people remain in the company: those who have nowhere else to go, and those who have just arrived and are counting the days until they have learned the trade so they can leave too.

**This is no longer a business that moves forward. It is a field hospital where yesterday's wounds are dressed every day.**

Today, those who used to say "let them leave" are the same ones holding their heads in their hands because they cannot find a single person to take responsibility for one shift. They are the same ones who complain that the young generation has no patience. But the young generation does not lack patience for work; it lacks patience for arrogance. No one accepts any more to be treated as surplus in a place where they carry the main weight.

The lesson of this time is simple, but merciless for those who refuse to change. People are not raw material bought in bulk on the market.

The companies that will succeed tomorrow are those where the manager understands that when a good person leaves, it is not the employee who has failed, but the management itself. Because at the end of the day, **a business is only as strong as the people who choose to stay there willingly, not those forced to pass through that door only for a while.**
