There is a very familiar figure in our offices, hotels and warehouses, a character you find in almost every sector where written rules are missing. It is the person who keeps all the keys in his own pocket.

He is the only one who knows where the system passwords are kept. He is the only one who has the key supplier's personal number. He is the only one who knows how to print that special report at five in the morning and what magic move on the keyboard has to be made when the computer freezes. If you politely ask him how a procedure is done, he answers in half-words, with murky instructions, or tells you with a mysterious smile: "Leave it there, I'll do it later, it's complicated."

At first sight, this person looks like the unshakeable pillar of the company. He seems a tireless worker, the one who never takes a full holiday, who phones in from his time off to give an urgent code, and without whom the work seems unable to move forward.

But let us look at this behaviour without naivety and with professional courage. **Hiding information is not dedication; it is the silent hostage-taking of the organisation. And the person who refuses to document his process is not protecting the company; he is building an artificial monopoly to protect the insecurity of his own chair.**

Why does a person do this?

The root of this old vice lies in a deep fear of being replaced.

Lacking strong character and real professional skill, this employee or manager believes that the only reason the company keeps him on is the mystery of what he does. In his mind, knowledge is not a shared asset to be passed on so the team can grow, but private property, a secret weapon for survival. He thinks that the day he writes a standard operating procedure, the day he trains a new colleague or hands over a clear shift protocol, he has signed his own dismissal.

So he deliberately chooses to keep the system in the dark. He deliberately sabotages clarity, creating total dependence on his physical presence.

And what does this do to a business? It turns the company into a building of brittle glass.

All it takes is for this person to be ill for two days, and a whole department falls into panic. Orders are delayed, customers wait, mistakes break out at every link, and the whole management is paralysed by a trivial obstacle that could have been solved with a five-line instruction sheet hanging on the wall.

The heaviest damage is not just financial; the heaviest damage is the poison this mindset puts into the spirit of the team.

When hiding information is tolerated from the top, cooperation dies. Employees no longer see each other as comrades sharing a mission, but as dangerous rivals from whom every note, every file and every lesson learned must be guarded. The workplace becomes a marketplace of intrigue where everyone keeps a fragment of the truth in their head, waiting for the other to fall so they can prove their own worth.

True leadership, the kind that leaves a mark and builds lasting institutions, rests on the exact opposite principle: **Your real value is not measured by how irreplaceable you claim to be by hiding knowledge, but by how independent you manage to make the people around you.**

A professional with dignity is not afraid of transparency. He writes the procedure carefully. He documents every step of the shift, leaves the system clean, teaches others without holding back a single detail and makes sure the work flows without friction even when he is hundreds of kilometres away. He knows well that his professional security does not come from keeping secret codes in his pocket, but from the quality of his character, his ability to solve new problems and the humility to build others up.

Albanian businesses must have the courage to free themselves from this inherited trap. No business can hope to grow in a modern market if its operations depend on the memory, the mood or the egoism of three people who refuse to write down what they do.

The time of the little feudal lords of information has to end.

A healthy organisation has light, open systems, documented procedures and free people who know that knowledge only becomes truly powerful when it is shared with others. And the leader who wants to build something that stands the test of time has only one way: to break the locks on hidden information at once and set the standard as the only sovereign authority over every individual.
