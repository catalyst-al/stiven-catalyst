In any normal company in the world, the first day at work for a manager or an employee begins with the basics of the job. You are shown the operation, given the manual of standard procedures, walked through the supply chain, how customers are treated and the targets against which success is measured. You are taught how the work works.

But in a large share of Albanian enterprises, the real training has nothing to do with the shop, the warehouse or the process. The first and most important training happens in a low voice, in a corner of the corridor, where an older colleague draws you aside and teaches you the unwritten rulebook of survival: who is cousin to whom, whose aunt's son runs finance, which driver must not be touched because he comes from the owner's village, and which young woman in the office carries the surname that opens every door.

This is our operational reality. You are not asked to know the system. You are asked to learn the boss's family tree by heart.

**Let me say it without gloves. When a blood tie is worth more than the work rules, you have not built a company. You have simply set up a clan with a business tax number.**

Look at what this mindset does to a day-to-day operation. You may be the best-prepared manager, you may have built clear procedures to cut damage, speed up service or bring order to the shift. But that whole structure collapses the very second it collides with the interest or the laziness of a "nephew" or a "friend of the family".

In that moment the rule stops working. The procedure becomes a laughable piece of paper. Because if an ordinary employee is ten minutes late, they are fined in the name of discipline. If the owner's nephew does not turn up at all, or wrecks a whole day's operation, the manager is asked to look away, quietly fix the numbers and carry someone else's mistake on their own back.

This double standard is the poison that kills every chance of development. It turns the manager from a leader of processes into a court diplomat, who spends eighty per cent of their energy not on how to grow the work, but on how not to step on the toes of the family.

I have seen how this culture turns the workplace into a minefield. Real professionals understand very quickly that in such a place, merit has a concrete ceiling. You can carry the whole weight of the shop or the operation, you can raise sales and solve every crisis, but at the end of the day the strategic decisions are not taken in the meeting room on the basis of data. They are taken at Sunday family dinners, where your work is judged by people who have never put together a single process in their lives.

And then the question comes up of why our companies cannot get past the limits of a local business, why they cannot create standards that stand the test of time, and why they fail as soon as the first owner grows tired or steps back.

**The answer is simple. A surname can be inherited, but competence cannot.** Blood can give you a director's chair, but it cannot give you the knowledge to read an operational report or the moral authority to lead people who know the work better than you do.

A modern operation does not need tribal loyalty. It needs clarity of roles. It needs a system where the standard procedure does not ask whose cousin you are, where a mistake is measured the same for every hand, and where the success of the shop or the warehouse is credited to the one who produced it with their sweat.

Until Albanian business separates the family dinner table from the table where the work is run, we will keep seeing the same sad picture: companies large in appearance but small in mindset, where the capable work in silence until they find a way out, while the relatives stay behind to share the shadow of a business they do not know how to keep on its feet.
