When we talk about injustice, authoritarianism or bad management, our imagination goes straight to the big figures. We think of the arrogant politician on television, the rich owner in his armoured office or the general manager who makes decisions with a stroke of the pen.

But anyone who has lived even a single day in the real world, every citizen who has waited at a counter and every employee who has set foot on the floor of a warehouse or a shop, knows another truth well: the most everyday, bitterest and most poisonous humiliation does not come from the top. It comes from the middle. It comes from the small man to whom fate, chance or a weak system has given a negligible scrap of power over someone else's life.

Let me say this with complete clarity. **Power does not corrupt a person; power simply pulls back the curtain. It gives permission to show openly what was hiding inside him before he sat down in the chair.**

Watch this phenomenon carefully. It appears wherever there is a procedure and an obstacle.

It is the guard at the gate who deliberately leaves you in the rain for five minutes before raising the barrier, just to show you that he holds the lever. It is the office clerk who keeps your file on the desk for three days, knowing the stamp takes three seconds, but who enjoys watching you wait anxiously in the corridor. It is the new supervisor who yesterday complained alongside you about the company's injustices, but who, as soon as he is handed a checklist and a radio on his belt, forgets every word, changes his tone of voice and begins to treat his former colleagues as born culprits.

Why does this transformation happen so quickly and so ugly?

It happens because a person without inner character perceives power not as a responsibility to serve, but as a historic chance for revenge. All his life he has felt unimportant, invisible and trampled on by others. And the first second the system gives him the smallest authority over another person's fate, he does not use it to make the process easier. He uses it to make up for all his old insecurities.

For him, every obstacle he can put in your way is proof of his strength. Every “no” he gives you with indifference is a way of proving to himself that today he is no longer the one sitting at the back of the room, but the person who can decide whether you move forward or stay where you are.

This is the core of the tyranny of the small man: he has no vision to build, so he can feel his authority only through the ability to block.

In the daily operations of a business, this kind of person is a silent poison that destroys any culture of cooperation. He creates obstacles where the road is open, invents absurd rules that exist in no standard procedure and insists on submission from ordinary people. And the greatest damage is not simply the delay to the work; the greatest damage is that he kills the dignity of those who work honestly.

A true leader is told apart at once from this parody of power.

A leader with character knows that his strength is measured by how many doors he manages to open for others, not by how many he keeps locked. He has no need to remind you of his title, because his authority springs from calm, from knowledge and from the fairness with which he makes decisions. He understands that the position he holds is temporary, but the way he treats people will stay in their memory for ever.

So the true moral test of any person does not happen when he has nothing in his hands and asks for justice for himself. The test begins the moment he is given even a tiny grain of power over someone else.

If in that moment you choose to build walls, delay the solution and feed your ego with another's suffering, you have not become strong. You have simply proved that beneath the cloak of the office or the uniform they gave you, you are still the same poor soul you always were.

And an organisation that wants a future must learn this lesson: never give the key to a door to someone whose only pleasure in life is to hear the sound of the lock closing in another person's face.
