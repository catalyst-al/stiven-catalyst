In San Francisco, in those glass towers with filtered air where thirty-year-olds in plain cotton T-shirts play with language models of billions of parameters, an existential panic reigns that is almost religious. They fear a cold, perfect and merciless intelligence that is coming to wipe human reason off the face of the earth. They shed tears for the end of order and predictability.

Late one evening, in a dimly lit café somewhere on the outskirts of Durrës – one of those places where cigarette smoke mixes with the damp smell of the sea and the noise of an old engine – I saw a young man with a scratched laptop nursing the same cold espresso for hours. He was not using artificial intelligence to discover new molecules or to write philosophical treatises. No, my friend. He was training that colossal neural network with the patience of a master watchmaker for a far more earthly purpose: how to game the algorithms of a foreign corporation to secure a few hundred euros by the end of the week.

And there, in that simple scene, the great Albanian paradox reveals itself in full.

The civilised world is trembling before technology because it built its empire on an edifice of rules, procedures and precise measurement. And now the machine is beating it at precisely its own game of logic. But the Albanian? The Albanian has never survived thanks to rules. Historically he has survived by mocking them and by finding the hidden side path.

How can an algorithm built on probability be imposed on a man whose decisions are guided not by equations but by spite, by the instinct to survive and by ties of blood? For centuries they have loaded onto his back empires, royal decrees, iron ideological dogmas and cold international manuals. And every time, his answer has not been surrender but instant adaptation. Given the chance, the Albanian would try to bribe the binary code itself.

But this struggle is not a clean triumph; it carries a dark and tragic trap. The real danger for him in this age is not that a machine will steal his mind, but that he will remain stuck in his oldest vice: using the tools of the future with the mindset of a medieval bandit. It is a deep spiritual degradation to reduce the highest intelligence human history has ever seen to a mere instrument for refining the next swindle, instead of finally building something that stands up to time.

And yet, if the world ever faces a total collapse in which the digital systems fail and civilisation desperately searches for a user manual, I know very well who will be left standing. It will not be the technocrat surrounded by frozen servers. It will be that Albanian who, with no electricity and no internet, will find a way to wire up a rusty generator, light a cigarette and smile, saying that things could have gone worse.
