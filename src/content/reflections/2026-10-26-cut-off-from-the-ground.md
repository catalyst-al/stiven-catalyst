There is a particular illness that strikes large structures when they grow quickly, a kind of elegant blindness that is caused not by a lack of intelligence, but by the complete loss of contact with everyday gravity.

Picture a meeting room on the top floor of a modern building. The glass table gleams, the air conditioning keeps a perfect temperature and bright-coloured charts appear on the big screen. The language spoken there is sophisticated: cost optimisation, reallocation of resources, a two per cent rise in yield and the elimination of operational waste.

Everything looks clean, logical, almost mathematical. In that room there is no dust. There is no smell of fuel, no grinding of metal, no cold night shifts and, above all, no people with names of their own. There are only numbers on glass.

But here lies the tragedy of decision-making cut off from the ground.

The decision taken in that conference room in five minutes with a nod comes down a few hours later to the floor of the warehouse, the counter or the factory. And there it is no longer an abstract percentage; it is a heavy physical burden.

That decision translates into an employee who now has to do the work of two people, because a number in a table was judged surplus by someone who has never lifted a load in his life. It translates into a broken pallet that holds up a truck for six hours, because the board decided to save on the quality of the packaging materials. It translates into an angry customer shouting at the young man at the counter, who bears no blame at all for the delay, while those who designed this failed scheme are kilometres away, drinking their afternoon coffee undisturbed by the noise.

Let me say this with the full weight of operational experience. **When those who make the decisions are completely shielded from their consequences, error is no longer merely a possibility; it becomes a guarantee.**

The history of leadership and management teaches us that no real battle has ever been won by looking at the map from the commander's tent without knowing the mud of the field.

What happens when the manager does not know how much a crate weighs? What happens when he has no idea how many minutes it really takes to book an invoice when the system slows down, or how the human body feels after seven hours standing on concrete?

What happens is what we see all the time in our economy: orders are given that look brilliant on paper but are completely unworkable in reality. And when the plan naturally fails, those who invented it from a comfortable chair never doubt their theory. They blame the floor. They say people did not understand the vision, that the team at the base is slow, that the workers cannot keep up with the company's modern pace.

This is the arrogance of distance. It is the illusion that the world works according to the wishes of whoever holds the pen, and not according to the laws of friction, fatigue and simple human logic.

True leadership requires a completely different act: it requires humility before reality.

It requires the manager to put on plain clothes, come down the stairs regularly and stand alongside his people at the hardest moment of the shift. Not to carry out angry inspections, nor to catch people out, nor to frighten the staff by watching from a distance, but to see with his own eyes, touch with his own hands and understand where the mechanism really gets stuck.

An open-minded leader knows a secret that the textbooks often overlook: the person driving the forklift in the warehouse, the driver steering through traffic or the employee greeting the first customer at two in the morning know things about the operation that no expensive consultant and no table can ever teach.

The real knowledge of an organisation is not kept in the archives of head office; it pulses in the living experience of those who face the problem every second.

If we want to build businesses, institutions and a state that stand the test of time and do not crumble at the first blow, we must give up this culture of shadows and cold distance.

Good decisions are not born of isolation at the top of the castle, but of a daily, unshakeable connection with the ground. And the greatest leader is not the one who reads the statistics on the screen most elegantly, but the one who has the wisdom and the character never to forget that behind every number changed with a stroke of the pen there is a human being, with effort, with physical limits and with a dignity that can never be turned into just a figure in an office.
