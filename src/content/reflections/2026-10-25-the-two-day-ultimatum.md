Picture for a moment eight full hours on your feet, on the cold floor of a shop where the air smells of dust and cleaning products. This is not only about the everyday work. It is about a rhythm in which mopping the tiles, straightening the shelves and cleaning every corner become a routine that wears you down physically before the day is even halfway through.

But that is only the visible part of the ordeal.

Where the real work ought to rest on a clear system, on technology, on data and on documented procedures, you discover the bitter reality of many of our businesses: there is no system at all. The warehouse or the shop runs on the owner's visual memory. You are not given a database, you are not given a clear guide, you are not given a single digital work tool. You are asked to remember thousands of items by heart, to know where every box is hidden and to be a living human scanner, simply because someone did not want to invest two coins in a simple management system.

And then comes the most absurd moment of this small corporate world.

When this employee, worn out by eight hours of cleaning and by the impossible task of remembering every detail without the help of a system, makes a small mistake, the punishment comes at once. He is called to account with the severity of a judge. He is told that he is not capable, that he cannot concentrate, that he does not understand the work.

In that moment, the manager forgets one small detail. The employee has been there for years, without any real training, without any institutional support. His only training was that famous two-day ritual at the start, when someone threw a few words at him in passing and told him in a curt tone: *If you get it, you get it. And if you don't, you're not cut out for this job.*

Let me say it openly, without a single pretty word to dress things up. **This is not a method of assessment; it is incompetence disguised as psychological pressure.**

A workplace that refuses to invest in training, refuses to build operating standards, will not put an inventory system to work and expects the employee to function as an organic hard disk is not running a business. It is holding people hostage through physical exhaustion.

How can you hold a person to account for a process you never once put on paper? How can you call someone lazy or incompetent who spends the day mopping the floor and trying to remember where the goods were put, while those who sit in the office upstairs have not built a single rule?

The two-day ultimatum is the last refuge of managers who do not know how to lead. It is their way of saying that the fault lies not in the lack of organisation, but in the weakness of the employee.

But the truth cannot be hidden behind mopping the shop's tiles. People do not fail because they lack the will to work. They fail when they face an environment where chaos is given the name of discipline and the lack of training is called a test of character.

A healthy organisation knows one rule well: the system teaches the employee, and training protects him from mistakes. Until our managers understand that warehouses are not kept alive by the visual memory of the exhausted, and that a two-day ultimatum can never replace a real culture of development, we will keep seeing shops where work has become a prison, and people who leave behind nothing but the dust they swept in vain.
