There is a particular kind of human insecurity that cannot be cured by bank accounts, luxury cars or offices panelled in expensive wood. It is the inner fear, hidden deep behind a harsh façade, that someone in the company might dare to know more, think more clearly or enjoy more respect than the owner himself.

In every developed market, the first rule of a wise entrepreneur is simple: find people who are smarter than you in their own fields, give them the right tools and let them do the work. If you hire a logistics manager, you hire him because he knows the supply chain better than you do. If you hire an operations lead, you do it because he knows how to build procedures and untangle the knots you have no time to chase. Their success is your success.

But in many Albanian businesses, as soon as you step through the door of the head office, this logic is turned completely upside down. There begins an invisible, daily and utterly barren war: the owner's fight to be, at any cost, every second and on every subject, above his employees and his managers.

He is not looking for capable colleagues; he is looking for a grey background against which he alone can shine.

Let me say this without any embellishment. **When an owner fights to outdo his own staff in knowledge, in authority and in ego, he is not defending the leadership of the company. He is destroying his own investment.**

Look at how this war shows itself in the everyday life of a shift.

If the manager proposes a clever solution, a process improvement or a new tool that saves time and money, the owner is not pleased. On the contrary, his face darkens. Instead of approval comes a search with a magnifying glass for an imaginary flaw, just to cast doubt on the idea. Because acknowledging the manager's merit, in his narrow mind, is experienced as a personal defeat. How is it possible that he did not think of it first?

And so the primitive need is born to make the other bow his head in front of everyone.

The owner will walk into the middle of the shop or the warehouse, interrupt the manager while he is briefing the team, and give an order that is completely absurd and against the logic of the work, just to see the looks of the people who understand who holds the whip. With every move, every heavy word and every arrogant dismissal, he will remind them that whatever diplomas, training or ideas they have, he remains the supreme master because he holds the keys to the safe.

This is the psychology of the one who wants to stay the tallest person in the room, not by raising himself up with knowledge and character, but by cutting the legs from under everyone around him.

And what does this sick war of ego produce? It produces a professional desert.

People with integrity, professionals who have ideas and the will to build something of value, tire very quickly of this absurd battle. They realise that their energy is being burned for nothing defending the truth from the boss's whims, instead of being used to improve results. And their choice is a natural one: they give up, take their knowledge with them and leave.

The only ones left in the company are those who have agreed to play the part of the subordinate. The yes-men who never object, the employees who hide every mistake so as not to spoil the owner's mood, and the people who pretend not to understand anything, just so they can collect their pay at the end of the month without being noticed.

And so the company turns into an isolated royal court, where the owner hears only the echo of his own words and fools himself that he is an unquestionable genius, while the processes stall, quality falls and customers dwindle day after day.

True leadership requires an inner greatness that many of these people have never known.

A self-confident leader has no need to shout to show who the boss is. He is not threatened by a young person's talent; he nurtures it. He is not afraid of a manager who knows the systems better than he does; he relies on him and gives him the freedom to set new standards. Because the strength of a true leader does not lie in the ability to humiliate those below him, but in the ability to lift them higher.

Albanian businesses will not escape their deadlock until our entrepreneurs understand this basic lesson: money can buy a building, it can buy a title in the companies register, but it can never buy real human respect.

And the day this truth is accepted will be the day our companies stop being arenas where the owner feeds his childish insecurities, and become places where work, merit and dignity move forward at the same pace.
