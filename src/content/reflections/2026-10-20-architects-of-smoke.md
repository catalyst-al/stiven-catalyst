There is a dangerous illusion that has taken root in the offices, institutions and corporations of Albania today. It is the idea that noise is work, that presence is performance, and that a colourful chart on the wall is enough to cover a system that is rotting from within.

If you walk into an ordinary management meeting in Tirana today, you will see a perfect spectacle. You will see well-tailored suits, screens glowing with growth charts, words borrowed from the vocabulary of international consultancies, and a feverish rush to show that something big is happening. It is a carefully built theatre in which every actor knows their part: the aim is not to solve the problem, but to create the impression that the work never stops.

**But let me be clear. Motion is not progress. Noise is not a result. And theatre is not management.**

For years we have confused authority with leadership. In our working culture the manager is still seen as the person who shouts loudest at the end of the corridor, who demands accountability without ever providing the right tools, and who measures an employee's worth by the hours spent in the chair, not by the weight of what they produce. It is a kind of management that feeds on micromanagement because it does not know how to build trust, and that produces chaos because in chaos no one can measure the manager's incompetence.

Look at what happens when the lights in the meeting room go out and we go down to where the real work is done.

In the warehouse, at the counter, in the field, on the night shift where no one takes photos for social media, the system does not work. It is held up only by improvisation and by the sacrifice of ordinary people. When a process fails every day at the same point, the Albanian manager does not ask where the system has cracked, but who should be punished. And when someone at the bottom fills that gap with extra effort of their own, up in the office it is recorded as a success of the management.

**This is the biggest lie of our labour market: we call development what is in truth only the exhausting survival of employees in the face of an absence of vision.**

I have seen this contrast up close. I have seen people with grand titles melt away the moment their chair is taken from them, because they never built a single process with their own hands. And I have seen quiet professionals leave this country every day, not always for a higher salary, but because they are tired of being led by people who know less and talk more. When mediocrity puts on the boss's suit, talent picks up the suitcase.

The bitter truth is that you cannot build a modern economy with a feudal mindset. You cannot talk about European standards when the work report is manipulated to please the ego of the owner or the director. When we lie with the numbers, we are not protecting the company or the institution; we are blinding ourselves as we walk towards the abyss.

But this reality is not a fate written in advance. It is simply the consequence of a wrong choice that we keep tolerating every day.

Real leadership does not begin at the conference podium. It begins in the quiet corner of observation. It begins when a manager has the courage to sit down in front of the raw data and say: here we are failing, and the responsibility is mine. It begins when we give up the culture of the "gleaming façade" and turn our eyes to the dignity of the process.

Albania does not need more managers who know how to pose in front of the mirror of false success. This country needs people who are not afraid to go down to where the work is hard, where the system gets stuck and where the truth has no filters.

Because at the end of the day, when the projector goes off and the handsome folders are closed, only one question is left that matters: **Did you leave the work easier, fairer and clearer for those who come after you, or were you just another architect of smoke?**
