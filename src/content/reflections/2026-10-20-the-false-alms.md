There is a sentence every worker in Albania has heard at least once, said out loud in the middle of the office or whispered as a threat along the corridors: “I am keeping you in bread.”

They are a few simple words, but inside them hides the whole twisted philosophy of our belated capitalism. It is the sentence where the contract dies, where the law disappears and where the relationship between two free people turns into a relationship between a master and a servant. When an Albanian owner says they are keeping you in bread, they are not talking about economics. They are demanding submission.

Let me be absolutely clear on this point. **No one keeps anyone in bread. A wage is not alms, work is not a favour and the employer is not a benefactor.**

The economy rests on a simple and fair exchange: the employee gives their time, knowledge, health and effort, and the business pays the price of that effort. Without the hand that lifts the weight in the warehouse, without the eyes that stay awake on the night shift, without the mind that solves problems at the counter, the owner's capital is just a pile of worthless paper and cold walls. It is not the owner who gives the worker bread; it is the worker's sweat that gives the business its life and buys the owner their luxury.

But why does this myth keep living in Albania?

It lives because for thirty years we have built a culture in which success in business has often come not from vision or innovation, but from the exploitation of cheap labour. Many of those who today are called entrepreneurs have not built systems; they have built feudal estates. They see the company as an extension of their own backyard, and they experience the wage they pay at the end of the month as a pain in the pocket, as money taken from them personally to be gifted to someone else.

This mindset produces a toxic workplace, where every legal right turns into a plea. Annual leave is treated as a gift. A day of sick leave is seen as betrayal. Overtime is called a moral duty. And when you dare to ask for what is yours, you face the astonished look of someone who thinks you are ungrateful for the “bread” they have given you.

I know what happens in a professional's heart when they hear that sentence. Something breaks inside. In that moment they understand that there is no future in that place, however high the title on their business card. Because you cannot build a career where you are expected to bow your head every time you receive the wage of your own sweat.

And here we are today, facing the great paradox. The same owners who yesterday wagged their finger and talked about the bread they gave others now appear on television and complain that they cannot find workers any more. They complain that the young are leaving, that young people have become lazy, that no one wants to work any more.

But the truth is quite different. Albanians are not fleeing hard work, because once they cross the border they work even longer shifts, in warehouses, in hotels, on building sites and in hospitals. They are not fleeing fatigue. **They are fleeing humiliation.** They are going to places where, at the end of the day, a wage is called a right and not charity.

The time of the masters who hand out bread is over. The market is delivering its own silent verdict through closed doors and missing staff.

The businesses that will survive tomorrow in Albania will not be those with the most money in the bank, but those that are first to understand a simple truth: people do not come to work to be fed by anyone's mercy. They come to build their lives with honour. And whoever does not know how to respect another's sweat will very soon be left alone in their big office, counting the keys of a business that no one is left to open.
