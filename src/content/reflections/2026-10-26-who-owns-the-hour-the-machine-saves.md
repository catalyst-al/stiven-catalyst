An hour of work turned into ten minutes.

Until yesterday, an employee had to open several systems, compare data, check documents and prepare a report. The process was slow, demanded concentration and left little room for other tasks.

Then technology arrived.

A new program, a few automatic functions and an interface that promised to make the work simpler.

During the demonstration, everything looked impressive.

Tasks that once took an hour were now finished in ten minutes.

The managers were pleased. The investment promised results. Productivity would rise. Processes would speed up. The company would become more competitive.

The employee too had reason to be glad.

Less mechanical work. Less repetition. Less time in front of a screen carrying out actions that no longer needed his intervention.

But a few weeks later, the list of tasks began to change.

Since the process finished faster, more cases could now be handled. Other checks could be added. Responsibilities that someone else used to carry out could be taken on.

The working hours stayed the same.

The tiredness did not necessarily shrink.

Only the expectations grew.

In the managers' presentation, the fifty minutes saved appeared as an improvement in productivity.

In the employee's life, they had never existed as free time.

And here begins a question that technology cannot answer on its own.

Who owns the hour the machine saves?

***

Technological progress has always been tied to a great promise.

To do more with less effort.

Machines helped us move weights that took the strength of many people. Computers let us process information that once took days of work. Automatic systems reduced the need for repeated actions and opened up possibilities that were once hard to imagine.

Today, artificial intelligence is extending this change to tasks once considered mainly intellectual.

A report can be prepared faster. A document can be analysed in a few moments. A request can be categorised automatically. A process can need less human intervention than before.

There are strong reasons to welcome these developments.

Unnecessary work does not become more noble just because a person does it.

There is no special virtue in repeating an action a hundred times when a system can carry it out reliably.

And a manager who refuses technological improvement just because he wants to keep the old way of working may be depriving people of tools that would really ease their professional lives.

But technology does not itself determine how the time it saves will be used.

People decide that.

More exactly, those who have the authority to change how work is organised.

And where human decision-making begins, so does the moral responsibility of management.

***

There is a silent assumption in many discussions of productivity.

If technology shortens a process by fifty minutes, the organisation has gained fifty minutes of capacity.

That may be true.

But it does not mean it has automatically saved fifty minutes of work in the economic sense.

The system may need checking. There may be costs of use, maintenance and security. Its errors may need correcting. The process may contain tasks that cannot be automated.

The speed of the demonstration is not always the speed of real work.

So before declaring success, we must understand how much time has been saved across the whole process, not only in one step of it.

Still, let us suppose the improvement is real.

The program works well.

Quality has been kept.

The employee can do the same task faster and without creating extra work for someone else.

Now the organisation faces a choice.

It can use the new capacity to serve more customers.

It can reduce waiting.

It can invest in quality.

It can give employees time for training, process improvement or responsibilities of higher value.

It can reduce the excess load that people have carried for years.

In some circumstances, the gains may also support better working conditions or higher pay.

None of these choices is automatically the only right one.

But all of them show something important.

Technology creates possibilities.

Management decides who benefits from them.

***

I do not think it is reasonable to demand that every minute saved by automation go straight back to the employee as time off.

A company invests capital, takes on risk, pays for systems and tries to stay competitive. A technological improvement must have an economic justification too.

Financial gain is not in itself something dishonest.

A business that fails to create economic value cannot promise long-term security to the people who work in it.

But there is a difference between recognising the need for gain and assuming that every productivity gain must be absorbed without limit by the organisation.

If a company uses technology to produce more with the same people, that may be an achievement.

If it uses it to eliminate tiring work and to create room for development, it may be an even greater one.

But if every minute saved is turned straight into a new demand, then we must ask where the improvement lies in the life of the person who uses that technology.

Has the work become safer?

Has the tiredness been reduced?

Has professional skill grown?

Has the employee gained more chances to take decisions of value?

Or has the number of tasks he must handle in the same day simply gone up?

Because freed capacity is not an order to be filled at once with load.

It is a chance to think better about how work should be organised.

***

One of the ironies of automation lies in how it can change the nature of human responsibility.

Before, the employee carried out the process from start to finish.

He knew the steps, the common errors and the reasons a result might be inaccurate.

Now the system does most of the work.

The person steps in when something does not match, when an exception appears or when a decision that calls for judgement has to be taken.

At first sight, this looks like relief.

And often it is.

But there is also a new difficulty.

When the machine processes the ordinary cases, the person may be left mostly with the hard ones.

Those that do not follow the pattern.

Those where the information is unclear.

Those where a wrong decision can have serious consequences.

So it may happen that the work involves fewer actions but demands more concentration and responsibility in each case that remains.

An employee can no longer be judged only by the number of tasks completed.

The complexity of the decisions he takes must be seen too.

Because supervising a system that runs automatically does not necessarily mean doing easy work.

Especially when responsibility for the mistake stays human.

The machine can produce a result.

But it is often the employee who has to explain why that result was wrong.

And if the organisation has not given him the knowledge, the time and the authority to verify it, then it has not empowered the person through technology.

It has simply handed him a new responsibility over a process he may understand less and less.

***

There is another consequence that seems to me more important than it is usually made out to be.

The gradual loss of the ability to do the work without the machine.

If an employee no longer carries out certain actions, over time he may lose the practice that helped him understand them.

If a system keeps taking the ordinary decisions, people may have fewer chances to develop their own judgement.

And if the organisation trains employees only to use the interface, without teaching them the process behind it, then it is creating a dependence that may become a problem on the day the technology fails.

This is not a reason to go back to manual work.

It is a reason to take training seriously.

Automation must free people from needless actions without depriving them of the knowledge they need to understand and check the results.

A good system must not produce employees who know only where to click.

It must help them develop skills that stay valuable even when the program changes.

Because technology can be an extraordinary tool for extending human ability.

But if it is used without thinking about the development of people, it can create professionals ever more dependent on tools they do not understand.

***

Then comes an even more sensitive matter.

What happens to the people whose work is no longer needed in the same form?

It is easy to talk about technological transformation from a conference room.

To show charts, present objectives and explain that the organisation is entering a new phase of efficiency.

But for the employee who has spent years developing a particular skill, the change may mean something quite different.

The knowledge that once gave him professional security may no longer be in the same demand.

The experience that made him valuable may have to be used in another role.

And behind the words about modernisation lies a very concrete question:

What place do I have in the organisation you are building?

A manager cannot promise that technology will never change the need for staff.

Such a promise may be dishonest.

But he can promise that decisions will be taken seriously.

That people's existing skills will be valued.

That opportunities for training and moving to other roles will be genuinely examined.

That the change will not be hidden behind optimistic language until employees find out from a decision taken in their absence.

And that people will be treated with dignity, even when there is no solution that satisfies everyone.

Because technological progress may be necessary.

But how its consequences are distributed remains a human choice.

***

At this point, a manager may object:

“If technology lets me process twice the work, why shouldn't I?”

It is a legitimate question.

If the demand exists, if quality is kept and if human capacity can cope, raising productivity may be the right decision.

But the question cannot end there.

Because there is a difference between technical capacity and human capacity.

A program can run at the same speed throughout the shift.

The person who supervises it is not a program.

He needs to concentrate, assess, communicate, take decisions and handle situations that are not always predictable.

If technology eliminates some of the mechanical work, that does not mean every space created must be filled to the maximum limit.

An organisation needs spare capacity too.

For the time needed to check an unusual case.

For the chance that an employee can train a colleague.

For the room that lets the team cope with a sudden rise in demand without at once sliding into crisis.

On a table, that room may look like unused capacity.

But it may be one of the reasons the organisation stays stable when conditions change.

A manager who tries to use every available minute may reach a high level of utilisation.

But he may lose the flexibility that protects the process from the unexpected.

And that is a price not to be ignored.

***

Perhaps our greatest mistake in the discussion of artificial intelligence is that we keep setting the machine against the person.

Who is faster?

Who makes fewer mistakes?

Who costs less?

Who can replace the other?

These questions matter for certain economic and technical decisions.

But they do not represent the whole problem of management.

The more useful question is what work should be left to technology and where human judgement creates the greatest value.

There are tasks that do not deserve to consume whole hours of human attention.

There are decisions where experience, responsibility and an understanding of circumstances remain essential.

And there are processes where the best result comes from cooperation between the two.

A system can help identify a financial error.

But a person must understand how it should be handled, according to the rules and the concrete circumstances.

A program can prepare a reply to a customer.

But someone must make sure the reply is accurate, appropriate and does not create a promise the organisation cannot keep.

An algorithm can propose a more efficient distribution of work.

But the manager must examine whether it is feasible, safe and fair to the people who will carry it out.

Technology can widen the possibilities of decision-making.

It must not be used to make the responsibility of the decider disappear.

***

An organisation that wants to use technology well should ask itself some questions before celebrating the results.

How much time have we really saved?

How much of it has been used to raise the value we create?

Have we improved quality, or only added to the number of cases handled?

Are employees developing new skills?

Have the tasks become safer and more manageable?

Are customers getting better service?

And are the people who use the technology benefiting from the success it brings?

There is no need for every gain to be divided mechanically.

Part may be reinvested in the business.

Part may be used to improve the service.

Part may support training, professional development and more stable working conditions.

What matters is that the gain not be treated as a phenomenon that belongs only to the financial balance sheet.

Because an organisation does not consist only of capital and technology.

It consists also of the people who use, check and improve them and who take responsibility when they do not work.

And if those people see that every technological improvement brings them only a higher target, we should not be surprised if they begin to greet innovation with suspicion.

Not because they hate progress.

But because experience may have taught them that the organisation's progress does not necessarily mean an improvement in their own conditions.

***

Yet responsibility does not belong only to managers.

Employees too must be ready to learn.

A new technology may require changing habits, acquiring other skills and accepting that some ways of working no longer have a reason to continue.

Long experience is an asset.

But it must not turn into an argument for defending every old practice.

If a system can do a task more accurately and faster, opposing it only because it changes the routine is not always a professional stance.

But from the other side too, managers must understand that adaptation does not happen just by installing a program.

It takes training.

It takes time.

It takes support.

And it takes accepting that people do not all start from the same point.

One employee may learn a new system quickly.

Another may need more guidance.

That does not automatically make him less valuable.

He may have other knowledge that matters to the organisation and that the new system cannot replace.

The task of leadership is to understand these differences and to build a reasonable transition.

Not to use technology as a surprise exam in which those who do not adapt at once are treated as an obstacle to the future.

***

But I would go one step further.

If technology really helps us save time, then we must have the courage to discuss not only how to use that time, but also what value it has for the person.

For decades, organisations have tried to measure productivity as the ratio between the work done and the resources used.

It is a useful way of understanding efficiency.

But professional life does not end at the ratio between two numbers.

An employee can become twice as productive and still feel more consumed.

He may have better tools but fewer chances to think.

He may complete more tasks but lose the sense of control over his own work.

And a manager who sees only the rise in output may not understand what is happening.

Because a person does not experience productivity only as a result.

He experiences it also through the rhythm, the responsibility, the autonomy and the tiredness it takes to achieve it.

If technology improves none of these dimensions, we should at least be honest about what it has improved.

Perhaps it has raised the capacity of the business.

Perhaps it has lowered costs.

Perhaps it has created new opportunities for growth.

These are real achievements.

But we must not automatically call them an improvement in the lives of employees just because they were achieved through a new technology.

***

Imagine an organisation that decides to use part of the saved time to improve the work, not only to add to it.

Employees might have more room to train.

They might analyse repeated errors.

They might improve the relationship with customers.

They might take part in developing the procedures they use every day.

They might face less pressure in busy periods.

And the organisation might gain something that does not show up at once in the productivity column.

More capable people.

More stable processes.

Better decisions.

Less dependence on emergency interventions.

This is not a romanticising of work.

It is another way of understanding the economic value of time.

Because a minute not spent on an unnecessary task should not automatically be counted as a minute lost if it is used to build skills that make the organisation stronger tomorrow.

Just as an employee who has room to think is not necessarily less productive than one who is busy every second.

Sometimes, precisely the ability not to be constantly busy creates the chance to discover a better way of working.

***

Let us return to the fifty minutes.

The machine saved them.

The process improved.

The company gained capacity.

But these minutes did not come with a label saying who they belonged to.

Nor can the employee automatically claim that every technological improvement must come back to him as free time.

Nor should the company think that every capacity created can be turned without limit into new load.

Between these two claims lies the space where management must show its maturity.

To weigh economic needs against human capacity.

To use technology without turning the person into an appendage of the machine.

To raise productivity without forgetting quality, safety and professional development.

And to accept that the success of a system cannot be judged only by what it manages to produce.

It must also be judged by how it changes the work of the people who remain responsible for it.

***

At one of the next meetings, perhaps a manager will present another technology.

Charts will appear on the screen.

Processes will be faster.

Costs will be lower.

Productivity will promise a new level.

Someone will ask what the return on investment is.

Someone else how long the implementation takes.

A third how many tasks can be automated.

All necessary questions.

But I would like another question to be heard in that room too.

What will change for the person who did this work until yesterday?

Not only how much more he will be able to produce.

But what he will gain in skill, in security, in the quality of his work and in the chance to exercise his own judgement.

Because a technology can be extraordinarily advanced and still be used within a primitive philosophy of management.

It can eliminate an hour of mechanical work and replace it at once with another hour of pressure.

It can make the system more intelligent while the organisation goes on treating people only as capacity to be exploited.

And then we will have changed the tools, but not the way of thinking.

***

I am not afraid of a machine that can do a task faster than a person.

I am more troubled by the possibility that we will use it to avoid a question we should have asked long ago.

Why do we work better?

Only to produce more?

Only to cut spending?

Or also to create organisations where human time, skill and effort are used with more responsibility?

There is no single formula for dividing the benefit of technology.

But there is a principle that must not be lost.

The person who remains part of the process must not be regarded simply as the empty space the machine has not yet managed to fill.

He is the professional who can understand, control, correct and improve that process.

And his value must not be measured only by the number of extra tasks that can be loaded onto him once the technology has finished its part.

Because real progress does not need only machines that do more.

It needs managers who know what to do with the possibilities they create.

**A machine can save an hour. But only a person decides whether that hour becomes more value, more freedom or simply more work.**

And perhaps one day, when we have automated a far greater share of our tasks, we will not need to ask how fast we can work.

We will have to explain why, with all the time technology has saved us, we still have no time.

**Because if machines free us from unnecessary work, but we fail to free ourselves from the need to fill every minute with a new obligation, then technology has fulfilled its promise.**

**It is we who have not fulfilled ours.**
